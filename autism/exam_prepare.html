Part A — Key Content Extracted from Your Images

1. Behaviour should be described objectively

A useful operational definition describes exactly what a person can see or hear. It should be observable, measurable, objective, and clear enough that different staff members could identify the same behaviour consistently.

Weak:
“The student gets angry.”
Better:
“The student screams above conversational volume and strikes the desk with an open hand.”

2. Use ABC information to understand behaviour

ABCMeaningQuestion to ask
A — AntecedentWhat happened immediately before the behaviour?What was presented, removed, denied, or changed?
B — BehaviourWhat did the student actually do?What could I directly see or hear?
C — ConsequenceWhat happened immediately after?What did the student gain, avoid, or experience?
Core exam idea: The behaviour itself does not tell you its function. Look for repeated patterns in the antecedent and consequence.

3. Common functions of behaviour

Attention
Behaviour results in interaction, conversation, help, or other social attention.
Escape / Avoidance
Behaviour helps delay, reduce, or get away from a task, demand, situation, or activity.
Tangible
Behaviour results in access to an object, food, activity, or preferred item.
Sensory / Automatic
The behaviour itself appears to provide stimulation, regulation, relief, or another internal outcome.
Useful question: “What does the student get—or get away from—after the behaviour?”

4. Prevent → Teach → Respond

FUNCTION→ PREVENT→ TEACH→ RESPOND

Prevent: Change what happens before the behaviour so the problem is less likely to occur.

Teach: Teach an appropriate replacement behaviour that gets the same kind of outcome.

Respond: Follow the student's plan, maintain safety, and avoid accidentally making challenging behaviour the fastest or easiest way to get the desired outcome.

Functional equivalence: If the behaviour is maintained by escape, teach a break/help request. If it is maintained by attention, teach an attention request. If it is maintained by a tangible item, teach appropriate requesting and waiting.

5. Reinforcement and token systems

Your images also emphasize selecting practical reinforcers based on the student's known preferences and using a token system when appropriate.

ConceptWhat it means
ReinforcerA consequence that increases the future likelihood of a behaviour.
TokenA conditioned reinforcer that can later be exchanged for a preferred item or activity.
Continuous reinforcement / FR1Reinforce each correct target response while a new skill is being established.
ThinningGradually reduce how often reinforcement is delivered after the behaviour becomes stronger.
Practical point from the worksheet: Pick reinforcers that can realistically be delivered during instruction. A favorite activity that only happens on weekends is a preference, but it may not be useful as an immediate classroom reinforcer.

6. Task analysis and chaining

A task analysis breaks a complex skill into small observable steps. Baseline data using + and − can show which steps are already independent and which still require teaching.

ProcedureGeneral idea
Forward chainingTeach the earliest step in the sequence that the learner cannot independently complete, then progress forward.
Backward chainingAdult assists with earlier steps and teaches the final step first, then gradually adds earlier steps.
Total-task teachingPractice the whole sequence, providing prompting or assistance on steps as needed.
Exam connection: Chaining is appropriate for a skill made of a sequence of steps. It is not automatically the best procedure for every IEP goal.

7. Evidence-based practice and ethics

An evidence-based practice is supported by appropriate research evidence, not merely by testimonials, anecdotes, or claims that something “worked for someone.” The instructor's review also connects evidence-based practice to ethics and safety.

Recognize concern→ Stay within role→ Document facts→ Report appropriately→ Follow procedure

Objective documentation records what was directly observed or reported. Avoid diagnosing, independently investigating, or making unsupported conclusions. Follow the specific reporting and duty-to-report rules taught in your course.

Part B — A Simple Exam Framework

For a behaviour scenario

  1. Define the behaviour operationally.
  2. Identify ABC: antecedent, behaviour, consequence.
  3. Hypothesize the function.
  4. Prevent: change what happens before.
  5. Teach: give the student a functionally equivalent replacement response.
  6. Respond: maintain safety and follow the behaviour/support plan.
  7. Measure: continue collecting data to see whether the hypothesis and plan are working.

For a skill-teaching scenario

  1. Decide whether the skill is a sequence or a single/discrete response.
  2. If it is a sequence, create a task analysis.
  3. Take baseline data.
  4. Select a suitable chaining procedure.
  5. Identify the first target step.
  6. Use appropriate prompts and reinforcement.
  7. Fade prompts and thin reinforcement as independence increases.

Part C — New Exam-Style Cases with Worked Studies

These cases are new and do not use the original case from your worksheet. Read each scenario, answer the questions yourself, then open the worked solution.

Case 1

Attention Function — Art Class

Maya is 12 and works independently during art when her EA sits nearby. When the EA begins helping another student, Maya calls the EA's name loudly, sweeps pencils onto the floor, and pushes another student's paper. The EA returns, says, “Maya, stop,” and talks with her for two minutes. Maya immediately settles.

Try first: Write an operational definition, ABC, likely function, one prevention strategy, one replacement behaviour, and one response strategy.
Show worked solution

Operational definition: “Sweeping materials” means Maya uses her hand or forearm to move classroom objects from the desktop until they fall to the floor.

ABC
EA begins helping another student.Maya calls loudly and sweeps pencils off the table.EA returns and talks with Maya.

Likely function: Attention. Staff attention is unavailable before the behaviour and is delivered afterward.

Prevent: Make adult availability predictable, for example with a timer or a “your turn next” visual.

Teach: Teach Maya to say, “Excuse me,” or use an attention card.

Respond: Follow the support plan, maintain safety, and give strong reinforcement for appropriate attention requests so disruption does not become the easiest way to obtain attention.

Case 2

Escape Function — Writing Task

Owen is given a ten-sentence writing assignment. He pushes the worksheet away, tears the corner, puts his head on the desk, and says, “I'm done.” The EA removes the worksheet and tells him to take a five-minute break. This happens often during writing but rarely during preferred computer activities.

Try first: What is the likely function? What replacement response must serve the same function?
Show worked solution

Operational definition: “Paper tearing” means Owen uses both hands to pull the worksheet apart so that a visible tear forms.

ABC
Writing task is presented.Pushes paper away, tears it, puts head down.Writing task is removed; break begins.

Likely function: Escape/avoidance.

Prevent: Break the task into smaller parts, offer appropriate choices, use a visual schedule, and plan brief breaks.

Teach: Teach “Break please,” “Help please,” or a break/help card.

Respond: Follow the plan and avoid making tearing the most efficient way to remove work; reinforce the appropriate break/help request.

Case 3

Tangible Function — Tablet

Nina sees another student using the classroom tablet. When told to wait, Nina screams and kicks a chair. To quiet the room, a staff member gives her the tablet. Nina stops screaming immediately.

Try first: Identify the function and design Prevent → Teach → Respond.
Show worked solution

Likely function: Tangible access to the tablet.

Prevent: Use a visual turn-taking system, countdown timer, or first-then board so access is predictable.

Teach: Teach an appropriate request for the tablet and teach waiting.

Respond: Follow the student's plan and avoid teaching that screaming produces immediate access. Reinforce requesting and waiting.

Same function: challenging behaviour gets the tablet → appropriate request/waiting should become the better route to the tablet.
Case 4

Sensory / Automatic Function — Repetitive Behaviour

Ben repeatedly rubs a textured strip on his notebook with his fingertips. He does this when adults are present, when alone, during lunch, while waiting, and during free time. Adults usually do not react, no task is consistently removed, and no preferred item is delivered.

Try first: What is the most reasonable function hypothesis? Does the behaviour automatically need intervention?
Show worked solution

Likely hypothesis: Sensory/automatic reinforcement because the behaviour persists without a clear social consequence.

Operational definition: Ben moves one or more fingertips back and forth across the textured strip for at least five consecutive seconds.

Important ethical point: A harmless repetitive behaviour does not automatically require reduction merely because it looks unusual. Consider safety, interference with learning, participation, and quality of life.

If intervention is needed: Modify relevant environmental conditions, offer acceptable sensory alternatives, and follow the student's support plan.

Case 5

Ambiguous Function — Attention or Escape?

Lucas receives a difficult math worksheet. His EA then walks away to help another student. Lucas pushes the worksheet to the floor and yells, “I don't know!” The EA returns, sits beside him, and completes the first two problems with him. Lucas then continues working.

Try first: Why is it risky to declare the function from this one event?
Show worked solution

There are at least two plausible outcomes:

  • Attention/help: the EA returns and interacts with Lucas.
  • Escape/task reduction: part of the difficult task is completed for him.

Best next step: Collect ABC data across several occurrences and compare conditions. If behaviour reliably occurs when adult attention moves away even during easy tasks, attention becomes more plausible. If it occurs mainly with difficult demands and decreases when work is reduced or help is provided, escape/help becomes more plausible.

Exam lesson: Use the wording “hypothesized function” when the available data are limited.
Case 6

Operational Definition — “Disrespectful”

A support plan says: “Amira becomes disrespectful when she is frustrated.”

Try first: What is wrong with this definition, and how would you improve it?
Show worked solution

Problem: “Disrespectful” and “frustrated” are interpretive and may be judged differently by different observers.

Improved definition: When a non-preferred task is presented, Amira says “No,” “I'm not doing this,” or “Leave me alone” above ordinary conversational volume, or pushes the task materials at least 20 cm away from herself.

Why better: It describes actions that can be directly observed, recorded, and counted.

Case 7

Ethics + Evidence-Based Practice

A parent tells an EA: “Someone online recommended a special treatment. There isn't much research, but they say it can cure autism. We started using it every night. Sometimes our child becomes very sick afterward, but the website says that means it is working.”

Try first: What concepts from the final exam review apply here?
Show worked solution

Evidence-based practice: Online claims, testimonials, or anecdotes alone are not enough to establish an evidence-based practice.

Main concern: Student safety.

Appropriate professional response: Stay within the EA role, objectively document what was reported or directly observed, communicate the concern through the appropriate school process, and follow applicable duty-to-report requirements taught in the course. Do not independently investigate the family or make unsupported medical conclusions.

Objective documentation example: “Parent stated that the student receives the treatment each evening and sometimes becomes ill afterward.”

Avoid: Writing a conclusion such as “The parent is poisoning the child” unless that is an established fact from an authorized source.

Case 8

Chaining + Reinforcement — Packing a Backpack

Jasmine is learning to pack her backpack independently. Her task analysis and baseline are:

StepTaskBaseline
1Place homework folder in backpack+
2Place pencil case in backpack+
3Place lunch bag in backpack−
4Put water bottle in side pocket−
5Zip backpack−
6Place backpack by the door−
Try first: If you choose forward chaining, where do you begin? What reinforcement schedule makes sense for a newly taught step?
Show worked solution

Teaching target: Step 3. Steps 1 and 2 are already independent, so Step 3 is the first unmastered step in the forward sequence.

Initial reinforcement: Continuous reinforcement / FR1 can be appropriate while establishing the new response—reinforce each successful target response.

Token example: Successfully completes the target step → earns one token; after the agreed number of tokens, exchange them for a preferred activity.

Later: Fade prompts and gradually thin reinforcement as independence increases.

Case 9

Choose the Teaching Procedure

Two goals are being considered:

Goal A: The student will independently wash hands using soap and water.

Goal B: The student will hand an adult a “help” card when assistance is needed.

Try first: Which goal naturally fits task analysis/chaining, and which is better treated as a discrete communication response?
Show worked solution

Goal A: Hand washing is a sequence (turn on water → wet hands → soap → scrub → rinse → dry), so task analysis and chaining are natural choices.

Goal B: Handing over a help card is a discrete communicative response. It does not require a long chain of sequential component steps in the same way.

Exam lesson: Select the procedure because it matches the structure of the skill—not because the procedure is familiar.

Part D — Quick Review Before the Exam

Five sentences worth memorizing

  1. An evidence-based practice is supported by appropriate research evidence.
  2. An operational definition describes behaviour in observable, measurable, objective, and specific terms.
  3. The function of behaviour refers to the outcome the behaviour produces or helps the learner avoid.
  4. A replacement behaviour should serve the same function as the challenging behaviour.
  5. For a behaviour case, think: Function → Prevent → Teach → Respond.

Fast function check

If the student...Think...
gets adult interactionAttention
gets away from work or a situationEscape / Avoidance
gets a preferred object or activityTangible
continues without a clear social consequenceSensory / Automatic may be possible

One-minute checklist for any scenario

What happened before? → What exactly happened? → What changed afterward? → What function fits?

Then ask:

How can I PREVENT? → What can I TEACH instead? → How should I RESPOND?
