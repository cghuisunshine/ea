Reading One · TARGET Texas Guide

Discrete Trial Training

Verbal skillsGrade levelsCognitive levelAreas addressed
Nonverbal · Mixed · Verbal PK · Elementary · Middle/High Classic · High functioning (Pre)academic/cognitive/academic · Adaptive behaviour and daily living · Behaviour · Communication/speech · Social/emotional

Discrete trial training (DTT) is an applied behavior analysis strategy that focuses on skill acquisition by manipulating the sequence of antecedents and consequences. The main components of discrete trial training include instruction, prompting, response, consequence, and inter-trial interval.

Description

Discrete trial training is a method of teaching new skills, consisting of a series of distinct repeated lessons or trials taught one-to-one. Lovaas (1981) defined a trial as a "single teaching unit." A particular trial typically consists of four parts: (a) the discriminative stimulus (SD) — the instruction; (b) the response (R); (c) the consequence or reinforcing stimulus (SR); and (d) the inter-trial interval (ITI), or presentation wait time. In addition, an optional prompt (SP) may be used to help the child respond correctly.

SD→R→SR→ITI

Essentially, first the teacher's instruction is given. A prompt, cue, or model from the teacher may be provided to help the child respond correctly. Then the child responds to the instruction, either with help or without. The child's response is evaluated as correct, incorrect, or no response. Last, a consequence based on the child's response relative to a predetermined criterion follows. Afterward the teacher pauses a little while before continuing, to let the child know that they have completed one set and have moved on to the next.

Why it suits learners with autism

A significant amount of research supports the use of discrete trial training with individuals with autism in a variety of settings. Discrete trial training can help to compensate for challenges faced by this population, and is beneficial for the following reasons:

  • Tasks are broken down into short and simple trials that accommodate the needs of individuals with short attention spans.
  • It attempts to build motivation by rewarding performance of desired behavior and completion of tasks with tangible or external reinforcement.
  • Stimuli presented are clear and relatively consistent. The child is given rewards only for behaviors in response to those stimuli.
  • It teaches skills and behaviors explicitly (cause-effect learning).
  • The instructions given are simple, concrete, and clearly provide only the most salient information.
  • It can be designed to teach perspective taking and social cognition skills explicitly.
Cautions

Discrete trial training must be individually and carefully applied, since each child with autism exhibits unique combinations of deficits and excesses in various areas (Lovaas, 1987; Smith, 2001). Furthermore, caution must be exercised not to adopt this strategy to the exclusion of all other interventions. In addition, if discrete trial training is used as an instructional strategy, generalization of the skill must be considered.

Steps

  1. Instruction. A teacher gives succinct and clear instruction to a student. The use of few and consistent words is preferred.
  2. Prompting. Based on the student's learning level and the complexity of the task, the teacher gives appropriate prompts to trigger the desired response.
  3. Response. The student responds to the teaching and prompting. The responding behavior must be measurable.
  4. Consequence. The teacher gives immediate feedback and reinforcement after the student's response to enhance skill acquisition.
  5. Inter-trial interval. A clear wait time is inserted after a trial completion before moving on to the next trial.

Brief example

To teach 4-year-old Jin to identify colors, Jin and her teacher sat down at a table in a quiet corner of the classroom. After getting Jin's attention on task, the teacher presented Jin's "penny board," which Jin was familiar with, and told her clearly that if she got five correct answers, she would be allowed to choose a desired activity.

Three cards of different colors were presented on the table. The teacher stated the color of each card while pointing to the card. After repeating this sequence, the teacher scrambled the three cards and put them on the table for Jin to practice. The teacher pointed to a card and said, "What color is it?" Waiting a couple of seconds, the teacher prompted Jin by exaggerating the first sound of "blue," for example. When Jin gave the right answer, she received a "penny" and verbal praise from the teacher.

The practice continued to follow this structure of steps until Jin mastered the skill. The teacher recorded data on every response and administered reinforcement for each correct answer. When finishing the trial, they counted the "pennies" Jin had earned. Because she had earned five "pennies," she earned five minutes of play with a toy she had selected from her reinforcement menu.

Summary

Discrete trial training is aimed at manipulating the sequences of antecedents and consequences in a structured setting. Most often used in a one-to-one setting, discrete trial training contains five distinct steps for skills acquisition. Regular data collection provides detailed information to guide future instruction.

Reading Two · TARGET Texas Guide

Errorless Learning

Children with autism share common characteristics in learning. For example, they adhere rigidly to routines and tend to over-select and over-generalize responses to failure or novel tasks. Therefore, errorless learning, which limits an incorrect response in a learning situation, is ideal for this group of students.

Description

Errorless learning, a procedure introduced by Terrace (1963), is a type of discrimination learning that decreases or eliminates the opportunity for incorrect choice selection, therefore maximizing the possibility of a correct response. Simply put, errorless learning allows learning to occur with few or no negative stimuli. The theory behind errorless learning is that error responses have negative effects, especially for children with autism, given their rigid adherence to rules (Green, 1996; Smith, 2001; Smith, Iwata, Goh, & Shore, 1995).

Benefits
  • Minimizes the number of errors
  • Increases overall time available for instruction
  • Reduces the likelihood that errors will be repeated in future trials
  • Reduces frustration and the occurrence of inappropriate emotional behaviors by increasing opportunities for reinforcement

In errorless learning, children only learn the correct skill. That is, the teacher teaches in such a manner that students do not make any mistakes. As a result, they do not learn an incorrect skill that will have to be corrected or re-taught.

Steps

  1. Identify and teach the child the desired behavior.
  2. Identify prompts that will ensure success.
  3. Have the child begin to perform the response.
  4. Provide prompts to make sure the child performs the desired behavior correctly.
  5. If the behavior or response is incorrect, increase the prompt to make the child successful.
  6. Repeat the trial several times until the child appears to be able to demonstrate the desired behavior correctly and independently.
  7. Following a specified number of non-prompted behaviors, conduct a trial to assess the child's correct or incorrect learned behavior.
  8. Finish the lesson on a successful trial with appropriate reinforcement.
  9. Fade or decrease prompting as soon as indicated by data collection.

Brief example

Ms. Cooper utilized errorless learning techniques in teaching John, a 3-year-old boy with autism, to recognize his body parts. She asked John to touch the body part that she named. At first, Ms. Cooper provided a full prompt by taking John's hand and touching the correct body part. She gave John a small cookie as a reinforcer whenever he finished the task.

After three trials, Ms. Cooper faded the prompt by merely lifting John's hand toward the correct body part. When John successfully performed the task, he received a small cookie. When John failed to perform the task, Ms. Cooper prompted him through the task and provided a reinforcer. Gradually, Ms. Cooper faded prompts. After several trials, John could successfully perform the task with no prompts.

Summary

Errorless learning is a set of teaching techniques designed to reduce incorrect responses as the child gains mastery of a novel task. It has been contrasted with trial-and-error learning, in which the child attempts a task and then benefits from feedback. This strategy is an effective way to teach a variety of skills to individuals with autism.

Reading Three · Burt & Whitney (2018)

From Resource Room to the Real World: Facilitating Generalization

Eddie and Mrs. Claire

  1. 1Eddie is a sixth-grade student with a specific learning disability in reading. At his annual IEP meeting, his general education teacher reported that Eddie was not keeping up with his independent classwork.
  2. 2In social studies Eddie often sits quietly at his desk and pretends to complete the assignment by copying irrelevant text from his book. In math and science, he writes random answers without engaging the content as instructed.
  3. 3Even though Eddie has been taught how to request help by his special education teacher, he was not transferring this skill to his general education classes. Asked why, Eddie said he doesn't know when or how to get help from his teachers — they always look so busy.
  4. 4Mrs. Claire, his special education teacher, wonders why Eddie raises his hand for help in the resource classroom but not in his general education classes. "What can I do to encourage Eddie to transfer this skill to where it matters most?"

Many special education teachers have encountered challenges similar to Mrs. Claire's. Teaching a skill within the highly controlled conditions of one setting and promoting the transfer of that skill to a less-structured setting may seem like an unattainable goal. Yet this is the challenge presented by many IEP objectives that involve generalization of a skill acquired in a resource room to be applied outside of it — indeed, it is a challenge for any learning task where one environment is selected for initial skill acquisition with the expectation that the behavior occurs elsewhere.

The ability to generalize skills across settings is essential at all stages of life for nearly every learned skill.

Although generalization, or generality as it is sometimes called, is one of the most important outcomes of applied behavioral interventions (Cooper, Heron, & Heward, 2007), it frequently fails to occur naturally following initial intervention efforts. In other words, generalization is not guaranteed. The challenges associated with the lack of generalization were first articulated by Baer, Wolf, and Risley (1968); fortunately, progress has been made toward the development of systematic generalization promotion strategies (Osnes & Lieblein, 2003).

In 1977, Stokes and Baer defined generalization as the extension of behavior change across "different, nontraining conditions (i.e., across subjects, settings, people, behaviors, and/or time) without the scheduling of the same events in those conditions as had been scheduled in the training conditions" (p. 350). Generalization is observed when behavior change accomplished under certain conditions, targeting a specific behavior and with specific students, is subsequently observed in different settings (e.g., from a resource room to a general education classroom), with novel people, with new behaviors, and at a different time (maintenance or follow-up).

The Council for Exceptional Children and the CEEDAR Center published a collection of 22 high-leverage practices described as essential to promoting improved outcomes for students with disabilities; one of these is to "teach students to maintain and generalize new learning across time and settings" (HLP 21; McLeskey et al., 2017).

Stokes and Osnes (1989) organized the strategies first summarized by Stokes and Baer (1977) into three generalization promotion categories: train diversely, exploit functional contingencies, and incorporate functional mediators.

Train diversely

Teaching procedures that call for a narrow range of specific responses are not likely to generalize to new environments. Similarly, an instructional setting that is too repetitive and predictable may result in overly tight instructional control: a student is more likely to perform a learned behavior in generalization settings only if that setting closely mirrors the initial teaching conditions. For example, Eddie's initial advocacy training in the resource room consisted of Mrs. Claire presenting independent worksheets, modeling hand raising, then prompting Eddie to do the same; each time Eddie raised his hand, Mrs. Claire immediately attended to his question. Eddie quickly mastered this skill in five consecutive trials — but repetitive instruction in this case likely produced overly restricted behavior patterns.

Use a variety of stimulus and response examples

Stimulus examples are the prompts that occur before the behavior; response examples are the various forms of behavior targeted in instruction. To maximize the probability of generalization, it is important to incorporate a variety of both. The method of general case instruction developed by Horner and colleagues in the 1980s helps to formalize this process: it involves a systematic assessment of the various stimulus arrangements likely encountered in a student's natural environment and presentation of a sample of that range during instruction. It is not practical, or in many cases even possible, to incorporate every stimulus example a student will encounter; the goal is to efficiently teach a skill by minimizing the number of examples needed while maximizing the probability of correct responding when confronted with new but similar problems.

When implementing general case instruction, it is also important to consider the range of situations in which a new response would not be appropriate — overgeneralization. It is appropriate for Eddie to raise his hand for assistance during independent work with challenging tasks; raising his hand in the middle of group instruction for unrelated questions is not. There is no formula for determining the scope of generalization in every situation. If a circumstance arises in which overgeneralization occurs, teachers must simply address it by incorporating the scenario in another round of instruction.

Make antecedents and consequences less discriminable

A second reason teaching procedures may fail to produce generalization is that students often attempt a strategy only if they are aware that reinforcement for that behavior is available and likely to occur. During initial training in the resource room, Eddie raised his hand for every trial and received immediate feedback each time — the positive consequence was highly likely and highly discriminable. General education teachers, by contrast, frequently have 30 or more students at a time; a teacher might not notice or could not immediately attend to him. Therefore, Eddie must be taught to persist under less-than-optimal and uncertain reinforcement conditions. If consequences are unpredictable in the generalization setting, they need to be unpredictable in the training setting to ensure a higher probability of maintained responding.

Mrs. Claire decided to teach Eddie how to raise his hand, wait to be called on, and try again later if a teacher was unavailable: Eddie would count slowly to 30 while his hand was raised, and if the teacher did not notice, he would lower his hand and try again later.

Exploit functional contingencies

A contingency is the when-if-then relation that exists between the behavior and the antecedents and consequences. To maximize the probability that a newly acquired behavior may be demonstrated under different circumstances, teachers should be aware of the circumstances in which the target behavior is expected to occur in the generalization setting. If the generalization setting is not likely to reinforce the behavior, consistently reinforces a competing behavior, or punishes a newly acquired behavior, no amount of training is going to transfer the behavior change.

  • Contact natural consequences. Before any consideration of how a skill should be taught, teachers should first consider what is being taught and whether the skill meets the needs of the student and will be reinforced naturally. Students want to know how a skill is going to benefit them: "What's in it for me?" A best practice is teaching behaviors only if they are likely to contact reinforcement in natural environments — the relevance-of-behavior rule (Ayllon & Azrin, 1968). Mrs. Claire scheduled observations in Eddie's general education classrooms and verified that hand raising was the primary means most students used to obtain teacher assistance.
  • Reinforce generalization. Sometimes a student must continue to behave as instructed even if the natural environment does not provide sufficient reinforcement. Preference assessments, behavior contracts, daily progress reports and token economies can motivate the student, but must be used with discretion — any artificial reinforcement system should be carefully and systematically faded, when possible, to avoid dependence on the system at the expense of natural reinforcement. Mrs. Claire's preference assessment showed Eddie would like extra computer time, and she offered 5 minutes per question asked in class.
  • Modify maladaptive consequences. Teachers should consider behaviors already being reinforced in the generalization setting that compete with the trained behavior, and consequences that reduce, eliminate or punish it. From conversations with Eddie, Mrs. Claire learned that some kids get made fun of for asking too many questions in class, so she asked Eddie's teachers to monitor student reactions to his participation and assist as needed.

Incorporate functional mediators

Despite strong instructional design and delivery with carefully planned behavior management systems in place, generalized responding may still fail to occur. Mediation strategies may be needed to bridge the gap between training and generalization settings. Two procedures receive special attention in the literature (Stokes & Osnes, 1989).

  • Incorporate common environmental stimuli. It is recommended that the training setting be as similar in form and function to the intended generalization setting as possible. This may require altering the physical arrangement of the training setting to mirror the generalization setting. Incorporating common personnel among the settings may help as well: if a general education teacher or students from the general education class are able to participate in training, all the better. The more features the two settings have in common, the greater the probability that generalization will occur. Mrs. Claire scheduled time in the library to train with the laptops Eddie would use in social studies, and recruited Mr. Harris to model the traffic light signal system.
  • Incorporate self-mediated physical, verbal, and covert stimuli. Self-management interventions may serve as remote prompts to remind a student what to do: listing the steps required to solve a problem, a reminder card taped to a student's desk in both settings, electronic timers such as a MotivAider or a smartphone application, self-management checklists and mnemonic memory aids. The common theme is the transfer of the role of the teacher to the student. Mrs. Claire modified Eddie's daily progress report to list the steps for obtaining assistance, with a starred reminder about Mr. Harris's class, and taught Eddie to self-monitor — recording each attempt with an open circle, shading it in when successful, and presenting the results to the teacher at the end of class for verification and feedback.
How it went

Mrs. Claire sent Eddie off to his first class: science with Mr. Harris. After 50 long minutes, Eddie returned to check in. With his head down and his self-monitoring form squashed in his hand, Eddie approached. "How'd it go?" she asked nervously. Eddie smiled and handed her the form. "Can I use the swivel chair for my computer time?" he asked. Mrs. Claire saw three open circles and one closed circle next to Mr. Harris's initials, followed by about 30 exclamation points. It was only then that Mrs. Claire realized the reward of 5 minutes of computer time for every hand raise was way too much time.

Recommendations and conclusion

Programming for generalization is perhaps one of the most challenging tasks for behavior interventionists (Hayes, Rincover, & Solnick, 1980), because much of the behavioral process involved with generalization is unknown. The following recommendations serve as a summary to guide practitioners' efforts (Cooper et al., 2007, pp. 650–651):

  1. Minimize the need for generalization as much as possible. Access to a student's least restrictive environment is not just a federal requirement; it is best practice for generalization promotion. Conducting instruction in the environments within which a student is expected to perform the skill eliminates the need for generalization promotion strategies in the first place.
  2. Conduct generalization probes before, during, and after instruction. Close, continual contact with relevant outcome data (Bushell & Baer, 1994) is a hallmark of applied behavior analysis. Generalization, as with any academic or social skill objective, requires consistent formative data collection.
  3. Involve significant others whenever possible. Incorporating parents, teachers, staff, or other students from the generalization settings in initial instruction serves much the same purpose as programming common stimuli. The most relevant controlling stimuli from a generalization setting are often the people with whom our students interact; bringing those individuals into the instructional setting also teaches others to be more receptive to the target behavior.
  4. Promote generalization with the least intrusive and least costly methods possible. Avoid unnecessary use of artificial reinforcement. If a behavior is to be functional in natural settings, it must be brought under the control of natural schedules of reinforcement; whatever additional reinforcers are used to initially teach a skill must eventually be faded.
  5. Contrive intervention approaches as needed to achieve important generalized outcomes. If generalization promotion is the ultimate goal, teachers should employ whatever strategies are necessary within the categories of train diversely, exploit functional contingencies, and mediate generalization.

The ability of teachers' efforts to produce generalization outcomes is crucial to the development of independent learners capable of succeeding in the real world.

Naturalistic Teaching

Overview: what we know

Early behavioral treatments for individuals with autism used basic principles of learning to create highly effective teaching interactions. Their success was largely a result of highly structured training procedures, developed specifically to maximize learning potential: distractions were minimized, prompts were delivered in a precise sequence, practices were massed, and consequences were delivered in a reliable, immediate, and frequent manner. These tightly controlled procedures, known as discrete trial training, have been found to be quite effective at teaching new and complex skills, and led to impressive gains in language, social, and adaptive skills for many individuals with autism.

Unfortunately, these highly structured approaches were not without problems. Of significant concern was the fact that many newly acquired skills would be exhibited in the highly structured learning environment but not in the less structured natural environment. The skills learned in controlled training settings did not automatically transfer, or generalize, to other important settings (regular classroom, playground), other important people (peers, parents), or other important tasks (play, daily living tasks). For example, a child who had learned to say "truck" when presented with a picture of a truck in a tightly controlled treatment setting would not necessarily say "truck" when he wanted access to his toy truck at home.

Over recent years, naturalistic teaching has emerged as a way to address this problem with generalization. Naturalistic teaching promotes generalization of skills to the natural or everyday settings where those skills are required. Recent reviews have found that research support for this approach is extensive (see Allen & Cowen, 2008). Naturalistic procedures have been found to enhance the generalization of language, social, and play skills, and in direct comparisons with more structured approaches have, in many situations, been found to produce better generalization of important skills to the natural environment. In addition, because naturalistic procedures occur within the context of everyday life and activities, they can help make learning more fun and in many cases increase a child's willingness to participate in teaching interactions.

Getting started: principles and applications

To understand the basic principles of naturalistic teaching, a distinction must be made between two behavioral processes: discrimination and generalization.

  • Discrimination. When an individual exhibits the correct or desired response under certain stimulus conditions and not others, the individual is said to have discriminated. An individual who selects a truck when asked to select the truck from a choice of truck, cup and shoe has discriminated between different choices.
  • Generalization. Generalization occurs when that same correct or desired response occurs under similar but untrained stimulus conditions. The individual who can select the truck when the truck looks different — different size, color, or function — from that used during training has demonstrated generalization, as has an individual who responds correctly when the teacher or the setting is different.

Discrimination training can be a critical part of learning, because sometimes it is very important that an individual exhibit certain responses under very specific conditions and only those conditions. For example, it is important that individuals learn "stranger danger" and not to get in the car with anyone other than a parent or guardian — which requires that "coming when called" does not generalize to every adult who is calling. Likewise, individuals must learn that not all four-legged animals are dogs, not all liquids are juice, not all coins are quarters, and not all buses will arrive downtown.

Discrimination is problematic, however, when it leads to an individual being unable to perform certain responses under many different yet important conditions. An individual must learn to ask for help not just from the teacher but also from a boss, a parent, or a peer; to identify a jacket on the coat rack as well as in a picture; to count coins at the store as well as at school; to recite his or her address to a cab driver as well as a parent. When individuals do not generalize important language, social, and play skills from the training environment to everyday life, difficulties in adaptive functioning may result.

Guidelines for implementation

There is no single or gold standard naturalistic teaching procedure. Various approaches can be found under a number of different names, including incidental teaching, natural language paradigm, behavioral chain interruption, script fading, and milieu teaching. The following guidelines represent an integration of the most distinguishing characteristics of these procedures.

Teach responses that will likely create contact with natural reinforcers

Generalization is enhanced when an individual is taught a response for which the natural consequence is a reinforcer. Natural consequences are those that would occur naturally, without needing to be programmed by the teacher: a natural consequence to a request for a "truck" is to hand the individual a truck. The use of praise, hugs, or even candy as a consequence would not be a natural consequence of a request for a truck, and these arbitrary consequences would require specific programming by the teacher — and responding may then be limited to conditions where the teacher has arranged for that arbitrary reinforcement.

Zach and Mrs. Cole

Mrs. Cole has observed that Zach loves playing with cars and trucks during free play, and decides to target Zach's expressive language using the natural reinforcers available. When Zach shows interest, he is required to request to play with the cars and trucks and to elaborate by describing features of the ones he wants first ("Yes, Zach, you may play with the cars and trucks. Which color cars and trucks would you like?" or "How many would you like?"). She hands him a few and, once he is engaged, imposes the same expectations before Zach can access more or different kinds — request plus elaboration.

Allow an individual's interests to direct and pace teaching

Given the emphasis on natural reinforcers, there will be times when teaching cannot occur because an individual — through illness, fatigue, satiation — is not interested in the current activities or materials. Under highly structured conditions, a teacher might try to overcome this disinterest by introducing arbitrary reinforcers. However, because naturalistic teaching relies on natural reinforcers, teaching is not forced when interest has waned.

During free time, Mrs. Cole had planned to work with Zach on sharing and play sequences with cars and trucks, but today Zach followed several children to the kitchen area, where he seems interested in setting the table with plastic dishes, cups, and food. Mrs. Cole follows Zach to the kitchen, takes several plates and pieces of food, holds them within view, and waits for Zach to request them; when he does, she asks him to elaborate the colors, sizes, and categories (fruits or vegetables) he wants and then hands them to him. Later, Zach loses interest in the kitchen and begins pacing the room; although she had hoped to work on turn taking, she recognizes that because his interest is currently low, naturalistic teaching is not appropriate at this time.

Embed teaching within the activities of everyday life

Generalization is enhanced when training conditions are diverse and loosely structured. The goal is for the individual to be unable to tell where or when training is occurring, so that trained responses occur everywhere and with everyone. By embedding teaching within the activities of everyday life, diversity is increased by the inclusion of multiple "teachers" — paraeducators, parents, peers — across multiple settings (on the playground, in the hallways, in the backyard at home, during a bath, at the mall) and at various times throughout the day.

Mrs. Cole has observed that Zach loves playing on swings and slides, and requires him to request to go outside and to elaborate by describing which activity he wants first. She has recruited Zach's parents and daycare providers to do the same, and several peers to act as peer tutors: when Zach is engaged with cars and trucks, she prompts a peer to arrive with additional highly preferred cars, which the peer shares contingent upon Zach's appropriate request. At home, Zach's parents make cars, puzzles, and computers accessible only after appropriate requests and elaborations, working from a list of the elaborations being targeted at school; they also model pushing the cars, making car sounds, and turn taking, and arrange play dates with a selected peer who brings preferred snacks and puzzles that Zach can access by asking what is in his backpack. Back at school, Mrs. Cole occasionally sends Zach on an errand to the office, where the secretary dispenses information only after Zach queries for it and keeps treats in one of three tins, so Zach must ask where they are, request access, and elaborate on the color, size, or number he would like.

Incorporate prompts that can be transported to new situations

Prompts are most often thought of as teaching methods that help promote learning. However, generalization can also be enhanced by including prompts within one training situation that can easily be transported to new situations.

Individuals with autism often do not initiate social-communicative interactions in any setting, so prompting is often required. The mand-model procedure allows a teacher to initiate an interaction by telling the child what is required and then modeling the appropriate response: Mrs. Cole might say, "What do you want?" and immediately model, "Say, 'I want a car.'" She might also introduce a time-delay procedure, gradually increasing the time between her request and her model, giving Zach a chance to respond but helping him before he gets frustrated or loses interest. She could also use a script-fading procedure in which visual or auditory cues are provided in the natural setting: to teach Zach to approach a group at recess and request a turn on a swing, she teaches him to follow a written script, initially prompting and reinforcing script following in a tightly controlled setting, and eventually letting him take the script to recess on a small laminated card in his back pocket, where another teacher prompts him to look at and follow the script as needed.

Adaptations and modifications

Restrict access

One of the common obstacles to naturalistic teaching is that opportunities are lost because individuals have unlimited or only slightly restricted access to the things that interest them most. With some increased restrictions and a touch of creativity, many additional opportunities can be created. Mrs. Cole has arranged the classroom so that some of Zach's most preferred activities and objects are visible but not easily accessed: his favorite cars and puzzles are on top of a cabinet that only adults can reach. When he arrives each day his dessert and drink are removed from his lunch, so he must initiate additional requests to access them; his coat and boots are moved, so after a request to go outside he is prompted to ask a classroom aide or peer tutor for their location. At home, computer games are locked in a cabinet and the best DVD is always in a novel location, requiring queries and social exchanges with parents and siblings.

Interrupt chains

Although much of naturalistic teaching occurs at the beginning of potential interactions, when motivation is high, there are times when it can occur in the middle of an activity sequence. Behavior chain interruption involves an interruption of the chain or flow of purposeful behavior in the midst of a familiar routine. Zach's parents typically require him to request to play a computer game, but they have also purchased a timer that limits his time; when the time elapses, a 60-second countdown warns Zach of impending shutdown, his play is interrupted, and he must find his parents and initiate a request for more time. His parents have also taught him to make his own peanut-butter-and-jelly sandwich; once mastered, they intentionally arrange for him to periodically discover that there is only one piece of bread or a very small amount of jelly, so that solving the problem requires interrupting his activities and asking for help.

Keys to remember

  1. Naturalistic teaching promotes generalization of skills to settings where those skills are required.
  2. To find good naturalistic teaching opportunities, follow the individual's interests and activities.
  3. Learning does not need to occur just in the classroom or in tightly controlled, distraction-free environments. Look for opportunities to teach within the context of everyday events with everyday people.
  4. Not all skills can be learned through naturalistic teaching. Some new or complex skills may be better learned in a more controlled setting before taking that skill into the natural setting.

Prompting

Prompting is extensively used in behavior shaping and skill acquisition. It provides learners with assistance to increase the probability that a desired behavior will occur. Successful performance of a desired behavior elicits positive reinforcement, therefore reinforcing learning. Prompts vary from most to least intrusive, and prompting should be faded to avoid prompt dependency.

Prompting is a means to induce an individual with added stimuli (prompts) to perform a desired behavior. A prompt is like a cue or support to encourage a desired behavior that otherwise does not occur — in other words, a prompt is an antecedent that is provided when an ordinary antecedent is ineffective.

The prompt hierarchy

Prompt (most → least intrusive)Description and example
Full physical assistanceThe teacher uses "hand-over-hand" support to aid the child in completing a task — when teaching the child to pick up a cup, the teacher takes the child's hand and guides him to pick it up.
Partial physical assistanceThe teacher provides partial physical assistance — when teaching the child to pick up the cup, the teacher guides the child's hand to the cup by tapping his elbow.
Full modelThe teacher models the desired behavior — when teaching the child how to clap, the teacher claps while telling the child to clap.
Partial modelThe teacher models only part of the desired behavior — when teaching the child how to clap, the teacher puts his hands in front of himself, but does not actually clap.
Full verbal promptThe teacher verbally models the desired behavior — when teaching the child to expressively label "car," the teacher asks, "What is it? Say car."
Partial verbal modelThe teacher verbally models only part of the desired behavior — "What is it? Say c___."
Gestural promptThe teacher uses a physical gesture to encourage the desired behavior — when teaching the function of an object, the teacher says, "What do you drink with?" while holding his hand to his mouth, shaping it like a cup.
Positional promptThe teacher places the target item in a location that is closer to the child — when teaching the child to label "toy," the teacher places the toy closest to the child.

Time-delay or prompt-delay techniques

This instructional procedure is proven to be effective, especially for children with autism (Walker, 2008). When teaching a novel task, time delay is used to transfer the stimulus control from a controlling prompt to a natural prompt by placing varying amounts of time between a controlling prompt and a natural prompt. Given different lengths of time delay, time delay strategies are categorized into constant time delay (CTD) — a standard time delay — and progressive time delay (PTD) — a graduated delay. The procedures begin with a zero-second delay trial, meaning the controlling prompt is presented with the task instruction at the same time without any delay in between. Gradually, to fade the prompt, time delay is increased between the natural prompt (task direction) and the controlling prompt.

Choosing and fading prompts

Not all prompts in the hierarchy need to be used when teaching a skill. Prompts should be chosen based on which ones are most effective for a particular child. Prompts should be faded systematically and as quickly as possible to avoid prompt dependency. Overall, the goal of using prompts is to help the child independently perform the desired behavior.

Steps

  1. Identify the least intrusive prompt. Choose a prompt that is necessary for a correct response to occur.
  2. Give differential reinforcement. After a correct response, give appropriate reinforcement that is equivalent to the level of performance independence.
  3. Fade the prompt. After the child masters a skill, gradually move the prompt away or replace it with a less intrusive prompt.

Brief example

Josh was learning to match objects with names. The teacher started with verbal and gestural prompts. She said, "It is time for drawing, and we need some crayons to draw with." She looked at the crayons on the table and pointed at them, saying, "These are crayons." Then she pointed at the crayons and asked Josh, "What are these?" After Josh responded "Crayons," the teacher nodded her head and said, "These are crayons! Good job, Josh!"

To fade prompts, the teacher gradually moved from verbal and gestural prompts to only positional prompts. For example, the next time the teacher placed the crayons on the table near Josh, she said, "We need some crayons to draw with. Josh, show me the crayons." Josh pointed to the crayons correctly and received a point on his token board.

Summary

Prompting is used to increase desired behaviors and skill acquisition. When delivering prompts, the instructor should be mindful of the student's learning level and fading considerations. Reinforcement should occur after a correct response and should be contingent upon the level of performance independence desired.

Response Prompting as an ABA-Based Instructional Approach

Two teachers with the same question

  1. 1Ms. Creasy, a special education teacher, wants to teach listening comprehension to high school students with severe disabilities who use alternative or augmentative communication (AAC) devices. She hopes her instruction will help students participate in a large-group lesson focused on reading and comprehending literature, and wonders about the most effective instructional strategy.
  2. 2Mr. Jin has peer tutors assigned to work with high school students with moderate intellectual disability. The peer tutors attend a physical education class with the students, and Mr. Jin wonders if there is a simple but effective strategy he can teach them to use during the class.

It would be wonderful if all instruction could be errorless. Students who seldom make errors receive more positive feedback from instructors — "You did a great job! I am so proud of you!" rather than "I'm sorry, but that is not the correct answer." Further, praise and other reinforcers increase the likelihood that a student will make the correct response in the future (Cooper, Heron, & Heward, 2007). On the other hand, students who make more errors are likely to practice their errors, allowing them to become ingrained (Collins, 2012). Using systematic instruction based on the provision of prompts is one way to increase correct responses while decreasing incorrect responses.

Response-prompting procedures are a specific type of systematic instruction that reflects the principles of applied behavior analysis.

Response-prompting instruction

All response-prompting procedures are based on conducting instructional trials, which consist of an attentional cue, an instructional stimulus (task direction), a response, and a consequence.

  • Attentional cue. Can be general ("Let's begin instruction") or specific ("If you are ready to learn, put your hand in the air"), and draws the student's attention to the instructor or the materials. This requires an attentional response that can be general (eye contact) or specific (hand in the air) and indicates the student's attention has been secured.
  • Instructional stimulus. A task direction that evokes a specific response; it may be verbal, signed, written, or pictorial.
  • Controlling prompt. To ensure that a student performs a correct response, the instructor inserts a controlling prompt between the stimulus and the student's response — the amount of assistance that a student needs to perform a correct response and receive a positive consequence. Controlling prompts are individualized for each student and each type of task: students who cannot follow a verbal direction ("Enter the number 5 in the calculator") may require a model prompt, and students who cannot imitate a model may require a physical prompt. Prompts are faded from instruction when the student has learned the correct response.
  • Consequence. Each student response should be followed by an appropriate consequence — reinforcers such as praise, a fist bump, or a smiley face for correct responses; corrective feedback, such as stating the correct verbal response or modeling the correct step, for incorrect responses.

Prompting can facilitate appropriate behavior by increasing the pace of instruction, which is more likely to maintain a student's attention and reduce frustration when a student does not know how to respond (Collins, 2012; Wolery & Schuster, 1997).

Six response-prompting procedures

Researchers have identified six specific and distinct response-prompting procedures for teaching both academic and functional skills: graduated guidance, most-to-least prompting, system of least prompts, progressive time delay, constant time delay, and simultaneous prompting. There is a large supporting research base showing that each procedure works for teaching various content, from single-step discrete skills to multistep chained tasks, with students of various abilities from preschool through adulthood, across settings (school, community, home) and instructors (special or general educators, paraprofessionals, peers without disabilities, family members, related service personnel).

How they differ. Graduated guidance typically uses physical guidance: the instructor makes a moment-by-moment decision on how much guidance a student needs, shadowing the student's movements and providing assistance only as needed, or systematically fading the amount of physical guidance over time — beginning with hand-over-hand assistance for a student first learning to use a pencil, and fading to just a slight nudge to the wrist.

Most-to-least prompting and system of least prompts are more systematic than graduated guidance and rely on a hierarchy of at least three prompts. In most-to-least prompting, the instructor begins with the most assistance in the initial session and systematically decreases assistance across sessions until a criterion of correct independent responses is reached; a student may take several weeks to move through the hierarchy. In the system of least prompts, the instructor begins each trial by allowing the student the opportunity to respond independently before systematically providing increasing assistance. The system of least prompts is especially suited to chained tasks — multistep tasks such as solving a complex equation or preparing a recipe — as it allows the student to complete known steps independently but wait for the amount of assistance needed on the remaining steps.

Instead of providing a hierarchy, the time delay and simultaneous prompting procedures rely on a single controlling prompt that will result in a correct response. During the initial session of time delay, the instructor assumes the student needs assistance and immediately provides the controlling prompt following the task direction — a 0-second delay trial. After a preset number of 0-second delay sessions, the instructor begins to increase the delay interval. With progressive time delay, the interval increases by one second at a time across sessions until reaching a predetermined interval (e.g., 5 seconds); with constant time delay, 0-second sessions are followed immediately by a constant interval (e.g., 5 seconds) across all subsequent sessions. In both, correct responses before or after the prompt are always praised; incorrect responses before the prompt are followed by a reminder to wait for a prompt; incorrect responses or failures to respond after the prompt are followed by corrective feedback ("No, the correct answer is rectangle"). The controlling prompt is naturally faded when the student reaches criterion of a set number of sessions of correct responses before the prompt.

The progressive time delay procedure may be most appropriate for students who do not know how to wait for a prompt, while the constant time delay procedure may be easier for instructors to implement, as it is not necessary to remember which delay interval is being used. With simultaneous prompting, the instructor always begins with probe trials to determine if the student has mastered the content; if not, daily 0-second delay training trials follow, and the sequence continues until the student meets criterion (e.g., three sessions of 100% correct independent responses) during daily probe trials.

Implementing response-prompting procedures efficiently

Response-prompting strategies often require less instructional time than other procedures because instructors do not have to spend a lot of time correcting errors. There may be reasons to use a hierarchy of prompts — some students may know how to make a correct response on specific steps of a chained task but need various levels of assistance on other steps, and an instructor using the system of least prompts provides prompts only as needed. Procedures that use a single controlling prompt, however, are typically more efficient because they take less time to implement (Ault, Wolery, Gast, Doyle, & Eizenstat, 1988).

Another way to make these procedures more efficient is to add content not directly targeted for instruction — nontargeted information — to instructional trials, and to use strategies that facilitate generalization. Teachers may increase the amount of content a student learns by adding nontargeted information as instructive feedback during trials (Werts, Wolery, Holcombe, & Gast, 1995). For example, the instructor can state related nontargeted information when praising a response: "That is correct — this shape is a square. S-Q-U-A-R-E. A square has four equal sides and four 90-degree angles." Another way is through observational learning, where each student taught in a group has the opportunity to observe the content being taught to other members of the group (Campbell & Mechling, 2009).

The instructor can also increase the likelihood that a student will generalize a learned response across settings, people, and materials, as first described by Stokes and Baer (1977) — facilitating generalization of shape recognition (a) across materials by presenting multiple examples (square boxes, square windows, square picture frames, square books), (b) across settings by pointing out square objects as they appear naturally in various school settings, and (c) across people by varying instructors. The goal is for a student to use a new skill with any material in any environment with any person, so it is important to periodically assess generalization across novel materials, settings and persons both while instruction is occurring and when students reach criterion (Cooper et al., 2007).

Countering myths

Despite the reasons to use response-prompting procedures, there are a number of myths teachers may believe that can decrease their willingness to teach in this way.

  • Myth: it takes a trained special education teacher, in a special education classroom, in a massed trial format, teaching discrete skills one-to-one. In reality, response-prompting procedures are more flexible and less regimented than teachers may believe — the research shows them used by teachers, peers, paraprofessionals and family members across school, home and community settings, in one-to-one and small-group formats, for discrete and chained skills.
  • Myth: students become dependent on prompts. There are strategies to decrease this likelihood, such as fading prompts and providing more specific praise or individualized reinforcement (e.g., time to engage in a preferred activity) for unprompted responses than for prompted responses — that is, differential reinforcement.

Two lesson plans

Lesson plan 1 — Listening comprehension (system of least prompts)

Core content standards. Literacy, speaking and listening: students will read and comprehend literature.

Behavioral objective. After listening to a read-aloud passage, each participant will correctly respond to 5 of 5 WH-comprehension questions by independently pointing to the correct response option on a response board for 3 out of 3 sessions.

Instructional context. Instruction occurs in a group format during a regularly scheduled social studies lesson following an adapted informational text read-aloud. Target students Brett, Quinn and Rhys are each seated in groups with three or four other peers, one of whom is a peer (or paraprofessional) trained to support their learning and AAC turn-taking.

Instructional materials. Data sheet and pen; adapted social studies text; response boards with at least four picture-supported response options (always including at least one "near" example from the same text); and a WH question anchor chart including the word, symbol, basic definition and example of Who, What, When, Where and Why.

Procedure — 5 trials per session.

  • Attentional cue: "Ready to answer questions about the story we read?" Gain the target student's attention with a verbal directive ("Quinn, look at me") and a gesture.
  • Task direction: individually ask a WH question relevant to the lesson.
  • Correct: independent response within 3 seconds.
  • Prompt level 1: if no correct response within 3 seconds, the communication partner rereads the relevant sentence from the adapted text (verbal prompt).
  • Prompt level 2: if still no correct response, the partner references the WH question chart to remind the student about the type of question being asked and then rereads the relevant sentence (verbal prompt plus explanation).
  • Prompt level 3: the partner models referencing the WH chart ("A Who question…") and then rereads the relevant sentence (verbal prompt plus model).
  • Prompt level 4: the partner physically guides the student in identifying the correct answer on the communication device (verbal prompt plus physical guidance).
  • Consequence: praise the correct response whether independent or prompted ("Great job answering the question!").
  • Data collection: the paraprofessional or peer circles the most intrusive prompt level (P1–P4) needed for a correct response on questions 1–5.
  • Maintenance: once criterion has been met for one day, praise for correct responses is delivered intermittently for two additional days; listening comprehension is monitored at 1-week intervals for 1 month.
  • Generalization: all students are exposed to multiple exemplars of adapted texts during social studies lessons; Brett is assessed on listening comprehension in other inclusive classes.
  • Behavior management: all students are intermittently praised for remaining on task; Rhys receives regular praise for staying in his seat or standing area while answering questions.
  • Variations: listening comprehension instruction can be implemented across subjects from science and literacy to job training; a preference question ("Why did you like this topic?") may be added to connect to personal experiences.

Lesson plan 2 — Health core content (simultaneous prompting)

Core content standards. Health: students will summarize the benefits and examples of regular physical activity.

Behavioral objective. When asked two targeted health questions 5 times each, Barbara will respond correctly 10 out of 10 times for 3 days: "Why is aerobic exercise important?" ("Aerobic exercise can make your body healthy and fit.") and "Why is stretching important?" ("Stretching can improve the range of motion of your body.")

Instructional context. Daily probe trials are conducted one-to-one by the special education teacher each day in the resource room, followed by daily training trials conducted by a peer tutor one-to-one in the school gym while walking laps at the beginning of each physical education class.

Probe trials (teacher). Attentional cue — "Are you ready? Look at me," or state Barbara's name. Task direction — alternate asking each targeted question five times each. Consequence — verbal praise for correct responses within 3 seconds; ignore incorrect responses or failures to respond. The same is then done for the nontargeted questions.

Training trials (peer tutor). Attentional cue; ask the first question; immediately state the correct answer and ask Barbara to repeat it; deliver verbal praise or error correction; state nontargeted information ("Walking is a good example of aerobic exercise"). Repeat for the second question, with modelled nontargeted information (upper body stretching postures). Repeat the procedure for 5 trials, alternating between questions.

Data collection. The teacher circles a plus sign for correct responses, a minus for incorrect, and a zero for no response; only data on targeted information counts toward criterion. If a student does not reach criterion on nontargeted information, this is targeted for direct instruction in the future.

Maintenance, generalization and behavior management. Once Barbara meets criterion of 100% correct on targeted content for one day, the teacher fades praise (after every two or three correct responses) for two days; after two weeks, Barbara is reassessed with the same questions. Generalization is assessed by having peers and parents ask the same questions across settings. The peer tutor and teacher periodically praise Barbara for paying attention.

Four guidelines for use

  1. Select a procedure that is the best fit for the student and the task. Procedures that initially use more intrusive prompts and provide less opportunity for independence (graduated guidance, most-to-least prompting) may be better suited to young children or students with more significant disabilities, as students are not required to wait for a prompt, be imitative, or know how to follow directions. Procedures that use a single controlling prompt (time delay, simultaneous prompting) may be faster paced and result in less frustration for students with short attention spans. Time delay or simultaneous prompting may be better for discrete skills like math facts or sight words, whereas the system of least prompts may be better for chained tasks where the student can complete known steps but wait for assistance on unknown steps.
  2. If two procedures are equally effective, select the one that is most parsimonious or efficient. Applying the principle of parsimony (Whaley & Surratt, 1968), instructors should pay attention to what works with each student and which procedure is easiest to implement. If time delay and the system of least prompts are equally effective for a task, the teacher might select time delay because it requires only a single controlling prompt instead of keeping up with an entire hierarchy. Efficiency can be determined by which procedure results in the desired outcome in the smallest number of instructional sessions or with the lowest rate of errors.
  3. Let data guide instruction. Instructors should monitor formative data closely, consistently, and frequently — recorded on data collection sheets as instruction occurs and graphed immediately after daily instruction, so visual analysis quickly shows whether instruction is effective. If there is little to no progress over several sessions, the instructor can "tweak" instruction by using a more motivating reinforcer, changing to a more intrusive prompt, or considering whether the student needs to learn prerequisite skills or be taught a complex skill in more detailed steps or smaller chunks (Collins, 2012).
  4. Increase efficiency of instruction when possible. Efficiency can be increased by adding nontargeted information as instructive feedback, teaching in a small-group format to facilitate observational learning, and using strategies that facilitate generalization. When a student reaches criterion on a task, the instructor should do a quick assessment to see if the student has learned additional, nontargeted information; if so, future instructional time can be devoted to other skills. The instructor should also assess generalization across different and novel materials, settings, or people, and provide additional instruction if the student fails to generalize.
Summary

Response-prompting procedures are a form of systematic direct instruction based on the principles of ABA. There is a large amount of research to support their effectiveness, and the research base continues to grow. Although earlier professional literature focused more on functional skills taught in special education settings, more current research has included core content taught in inclusive settings. Response-prompting procedures are simple enough for almost anyone to learn and flexible enough to be used in a variety of settings with a variety of skills.
