Brief introduction

Applied behavior analysis (ABA) has its roots in the philosophy of modern behaviorism pioneered by Skinner, who laid the foundation in the early 1900s. As such, it is the practical application of behavioral laws — laws of reinforcement — formulated by Skinner to solve behavior problems.

ABA is defined as the process of systematically applying interventions based upon the principles of behavior theory to improve socially significant behaviors, including reading, academics, social skills, communication, and adaptive living skills, to a meaningful degree, and to demonstrate that the interventions employed are responsible for the improvement in behavior (Baer, Wolf, & Risley, 1968; Sulzer-Azaroff & Mayer, 1991).

Description

ABA is "the process of applying sometimes tentative principles of behavior to the improvement of specific behaviors, and simultaneously evaluating whether or not any changes noted are indeed attributed to the process of application" (p. 91).

Baer, Wolf, & Risley (1968)

ABA-based interventions have been popular with children with autism since the 1980s and can be linked to the work of Ivar Lovaas (1987). Numerous studies have demonstrated and documented the effectiveness of ABA-based intervention with individuals with autism. To date, ABA-based interventions have been used to support individuals with autism in at least six ways (Shaping Behavior, 2008):

  1. To increase behavior;
  2. To teach a new skill;
  3. To maintain behaviors;
  4. To generalize or to transfer behavior from one situation or response to another;
  5. To restrict or narrow conditions under which interfering behaviors occur; and
  6. To reduce interfering behavior.

Characteristics of ABA

According to Baer et al. (1968) and Heward, Heron, Neef, Peterson, Sainato, Cartledge, et al. (2005), ABA has the following characteristics.

Applied

ABA focuses on areas that are of social significance. Behavior scientists not only take into consideration long-term behavior change, but also look at how behavior changes affect the individual and the people around.

Behavioral

The behavior must be objectively measured and must change.

Analytic

The behavior scientist must demonstrate control over the behavior with intervention while maintaining ethical standards.

Systematic

ABA interventions and procedures must be very detailed so that other researchers are able to replicate the application with the same results.

Generalizeable

The results of an ABA-based intervention must last over time, across settings, and spread to other behaviors not directly addressed.

Data-based

ABA uses direct and frequent measurements to enable analysts to detect their success or failures so that appropriate changes can be made.

ABA-based programs

Many programs use some or all of the components of ABA, and sometimes are referred to as ABA programs. However, calling a program ABA does not indicate which specific interventions are used. Indeed, there is no single intervention called ABA.

Programs that are commonly referred to as having ABA-based interventions include the Young Autism Project or Lovaas Model (Lovaas, 1987); Princeton Child Development Institute (Fenske, Zalenski, Krantz, & McClannahan, 1985); Douglass Developmental Disabilities Center at Rutgers University (Handleman, Harris, Gordon, Kristoff, & Fuentes, 1991); the River Street Autism Program (Dyer, Martino, & Parvenski, 2006); Therapeutic Pathways (Howard, Sparkman, Howard, Green, & Stanislaw, 2005); the Walden Program (McGee, Morrier, & Daly, 1999); the Institute for Child Development at State University of New York (Romanczyk, Lockshin, Gillis, & Matey, 2006); Autism Partnerships (Leaf, McEachin, & Harsh, 1999); the May Institute (Luce & Christian, 1989); Pivotal Response Treatment (Koegel, Koegel, Shoshan, & McNerney, 1999); Alpine Learning Group (Meyer, Taylor, Cerion, Fisher, Moran, & Richard, 2006); the Pyramid Approach (Bondy & Battaglini, 2006); Verbal Behavior (Partington, 2005); the Eden Model (Holmes, 1998); the STAR Program (Arick, Krug, Loos, & Falco, 2005); Learning Experiences and Alternative Program for Preschoolers and Their Parents (LEAP; Strain & Hoyson, 2000); Project DATA (Schwartz, Sandall, McBride, & Boulware, 2004); and Gentle Teaching (McGee, Menolascino, Hobbs, & Menousek, 1987).

Summary

In short

ABA is a framework for the practice of a science that systematically applies interventions based upon the principles of behavior theory to improve socially significant behaviors. It emphasizes explaining behavior in terms of external events that can be manipulated. Techniques and principles associated with ABA include discrete trial training, errorless learning, incidental teaching, prompting, fading, and shaping. The effectiveness of individual ABA-based interventions with individuals with autism is well documented.

ABA in Special Education: Misconceptions and Guidelines for Use

The case that runs through this reading

  1. 1After returning from a conference, Chantelle, an undergraduate student majoring in special education, finds that she has many questions related to the use of applied behavior analysis within teaching.
  2. 2She attended exciting sessions describing the positive effects of behavior-analytic practices in special education — and other sessions in which presenters did not speak favorably about ABA, some even regarding it as a cruel practice for students with disabilities.
  3. 3She sought guidance from her advisor, Dr. Thatcher, to answer her questions and help her decide whether or not she should incorporate ABA into her future practice.

ABA involves the application of the science of human behavior to address important social issues (Cooper, Heron, & Heward, 2007). It encompasses a range of practices and procedures that have been well established in the research literature as effective and in many cases have been deemed "evidence based," such as discrete trial teaching, reinforcement, the Picture Exchange Communication System, and task analyses (Wong et al., 2015).

Researchers and practitioners in recent years have provided many examples of the utility of ABA for addressing educational and behavioral needs of students receiving special education services. For example, positive reinforcement has been widely used in programs designed to teach students in general and special education a variety of skills, both adaptive and academic (Alberto & Troutman, 2013). Research teams have applied positive reinforcement to teach communication, reading, employment skills, and a vast range of other instructional targets. Group interventions based on the principles of ABA also are implemented in general education settings to support all students, including those with and without disabilities. In fact, many highly effective practices in special education are derived from ABA — explicit instruction, response prompting, positive feedback.

ABA and ASD

Despite the widespread applicability of ABA for the instruction and support of students with a range of abilities, it has become synonymous with interventions designed for students with ASD. The strong association is most likely due to a confluence of factors.

  • First, the work of an ABA pioneer, Ivar Lovaas, demonstrated significant social-communication gains for individuals with ASD following early, intensive behavioral intervention (Lovaas, 1987). As a result, many intervention programs for children with ASD adopted behavioral strategies as their primary approach for teaching communication (e.g., picture exchange) and academic skills (e.g., constant time delay).
  • Second, within the past decade, an increasing number of health insurance companies began to provide coverage for therapeutic services to individuals with ASD (Baller et al., 2016). Although instrumental in helping individuals access evidence-based services, insurance coverage created incentives for practitioners to market their services specifically toward that population.
  • Finally, strong parent advocacy groups have played a role in promoting the use of ABA for students with ASD (Guercio & Murray, 2014).

Although there is a clear link between delivery of effective instruction and support of students with ASD and ABA, ABA's applications go beyond this population.

Dr. Thatcher helped Chantelle realize that ABA is not a specific set of interventions for students with ASD; rather, ABA is a scientific approach to teaching and learning that could be useful for teaching academic, social, and other skills to a wide range of learners.

Misconception: behavior analysis is only about data

Chantelle still had questions. She had heard ABA proponents were more interested in collecting and graphing data than a student's welfare; however, some parents did not believe data sets and graphs could adequately represent their child. Wasn't a child more complex?

Behavior analysis is particularly well known for its heavy reliance on data collection and evaluation. The reliance on data is justified by an ethical obligation to ensure students with disabilities are provided services that include access to effective interventions and therapeutic environments (Council for Exceptional Children, 2015; Van Houten, Axelrod, Bailey, & Favell, 1988). Specifically, the objective measurement of student progress is crucial for ensuring the provision of effective instruction because recollection and subjective impressions are unreliable (Fixsen, Phillips, & Wolf, 1972).

A behavior analyst will likely coach a teacher to track, in real time, the number of minutes a student is out of seat, because the teacher's memory might be influenced by a range of classroom variables — the behavior of other students, increased attention to the target student, focus on the lesson. The data are then graphed, visually analyzed, and used as evidence for determining whether an intervention is working. Using data to conduct objective evaluations of intervention effects on both targeted (e.g., in-seat) and nontargeted (e.g., talking-out) behavior is consistent with the ethical underpinnings of behavior analysis and special education. Thus, in contrast to misconceptions related to impersonal use of data, data collection is an essential aspect of individualizing intervention and support as well as minimizing subjectivity.

Chantelle learned that the emphasis on data collection in the behavior-analytic community was no different from current teaching practices like progress monitoring. She also realized the importance of data in making decisions about whether to intervene, to continue a current intervention, or to make adjustments. Most important, she understood that the data were not a representation of the whole student per se, but rather a tool to support intervention decisions and advocate for the student receiving services.

Misconception: behavior analysis relies on punishment

Punishment involves the delivery of aversive stimuli (i.e., an undesired item or activity) or the removal of preferred stimuli following a behavior that decreases the likelihood that behavior will occur again in the future (Cooper et al., 2007). Although there are many behaviors requiring the use of behavior reduction procedures (e.g., aggression, self-injurious behaviors, bullying), ABA has a history of intervention procedures that are considered crude and unethical by today's standards — including the use of procedures such as restraint and electric shock (Bailey & Burch, 2011).

Similar to other professions, there are some bad actors that misuse techniques in ways that are harmful to others. Recognizing the potential for human fallibility does not suggest that practitioners should not be held accountable for their actions, nor does it dismiss any instance of harm caused as a result of aversive procedures. On the contrary, the behavior-analytic field recognized limitations and acknowledged societal concerns, and new and improved practices were developed along with implementation guidelines (Axelrod, 1987). The field assessed and identified negative side effects associated with punishment procedures, and the behavior-analytic ethical code now includes guidelines for punishment procedures (BACB, 2017).

The discontinuation of practices such as aversive punishment can partially be attributed to developing more effective, efficient, and safer function-based interventions — interventions addressing the reason an individual engages in the target behavior. The individualized approach to assessment and intervention helps practitioners develop interventions matching the correct function rather than arbitrarily implementing generic and potentially ineffective interventions. Function-based interventions also influenced the shift away from punishment-based interventions to reinforcement-based and proactive interventions within school settings.

Historically, students in public school settings received reactive, aversive, and sometimes arbitrary consequences following target behavior (Sidman, 1989). In contrast, the shift away from aversive interventions involved teaching and rewarding appropriate behavior rather than administering arbitrary consequences following inappropriate behavior (Horner et al., 1990/2005). In fact, entire school districts implement programs based on positive reinforcement, such as positive behavior interventions and supports (PBIS).

Chantelle now recognized another similarity between education and behavior analysis: both fields included a history with punishment-based strategies, and both learned hard lessons from their histories. In her future practice, she knew she could avoid the use of aversive procedures by using function-based interventions to replace severe behavior with more socially acceptable behaviors.

Misconception: ABA is about compliance

From a behavior-analytic perspective, a focus on compliance poses problems for two major reasons.

  1. The term means different things to different people. One teacher may interpret student compliance as generally following a set of rules within a given context, whereas another may view it as a particular response to a teacher's directive. Both interpretations reflect potentially different skills and may do little to identify multiple sources of noncompliance (e.g., language barriers, inability to perform the requested task). Consider a new teacher who reports a student to be "noncompliant" during group work activities: it is unclear under what specific conditions the student is not responding, and difficult to determine whether the lack of responding is a result of not understanding directions, a lack of social skills, or a history of escaping group work through avoiding participation.
  2. The criterion is set by the person who benefits. Compliance is often judged by the individual who directly benefits from compliant responding. At best, this may produce inconsistent performance standards, making it difficult for students to understand expectations. At worst, it may present an ethical dilemma, as an individual's compliance may be more beneficial to others than to the individual (e.g., sitting quietly at one's desk instead of actually learning new skills). Further, in some cases, consequences for compliant behavior can put individuals at risk for harm and abuse (e.g., bullying behavior, inappropriate sexual contact).

Thus, applications of ABA should be based upon a person-centered approach, and practitioners should remain keenly aware of the dangers associated with teaching individuals, especially those with disabilities, to freely comply with the requests of others. Chantelle realized that using ABA didn't mean she needed to focus on compliance with demands. She could instead carefully consider goals and use ABA to help students exercise independence and decision making.

Misconception: reinforcement is bribery

Practitioners often answer the question of bribery after school consultation visits or classroom observations. For example, parents may ask, "Why did you bribe my child with a sticker for working when she should do it just because you asked her?" To which a teacher might reply, "Rewards, such as classroom cash, sticker charts, or point systems, teach students that hard work results in consequences, such as payment — much like for you and me."

Individuals with and without disabilities engage in a range of behaviors as a result of consequences that often go unnoticed and would unlikely be considered bribery. Mr. Taylor's paycheck produces consistent attendance at his teaching job, and Sandra's positive interaction with her peers ensures her selection of a seat at her friends' table during lunch. In both instances, it would be odd to suggest that the school's principal or Sandra's friends are using bribery to control behavior.

Positive reinforcement≠Bribery

Positive reinforcement is the introduction of a stimulus into an environment contingent on a specific behavior, resulting in the increased probability of that behavior occurring in the future (Alberto & Troutman, 2013; Cooper et al., 2007). Bribery, on the other hand, is described by O'Leary, Poulos, and Devine (1972) as the promise of a reward before target behavior occurs in an attempt to influence the individual's behavior.

In the example above, the teacher noticed a student had difficulty responding to questions in class, gave the student a sticker after she answered questions, and noticed an immediate increase in her attempts to answer. The teacher's hope is then to systematically increase the number of questions the student independently answers before receiving a sticker and, eventually, by pairing the stickers with praise and other natural consequences, that the student will begin answering for more typical reinforcers (e.g., positive feedback). However, bribery would occur if the teacher noticed the student off task and then began incentivizing the student by telling her, "Get back on task so that you can get your sticker," rather than providing a sticker when the student was on task.

Although some educators may be uncomfortable with the programmed delivery of reinforcers, they must acknowledge that some students do not respond for the typical "pat on the back" or some "intrinsic motivator." In these instances, teachers must consider adjusting their perceptions around "what students should do" and seek reinforcers that will serve to motivate their students. The goal of teaching is to create optimal conditions for learning.

However, behavior-analytic techniques also encourage using natural reinforcers (events likely to follow the student's behavior in the natural environment; Hineline & Morris, 1992) and teaching students to wait for rewards rather than expect something right away. Ultimately, it is best practice to teach functional and relevant responses that will access more natural reinforcers — such as developing friendships after learning appropriate ways to gain attention, or learning math skills that are later used to purchase goods and services. Contrived reinforcers (e.g., programmed delivery of praise, stickers, or tokens) become a problem only when we overlay them on prevailing schedules of reinforcement for students who do not need that extra push.

Behavior-analytic methods and guidelines for use

Data collection

Behavior intervention teams collect data using various procedures that are matched to a clearly defined target behavior of interest and the context under which the behavior is likely to occur. ABA does not dictate a "one-size-fits-all" approach to data collection but emphasizes behavior-analytic procedures that are doable and can be implemented by relevant stakeholders. For example, it may not be practical for a general education teacher to constantly monitor a single student for an entire instructional period. A mindful behavior intervention team will collaborate to design a feasible data collection method that will capture meaningful data.

MethodDefinitionExample
FrequencyA count of how many times the behavior occurredRecording how many times a student called out during math class
Percentage of occurrenceThe number of correct responses divided by the number of opportunities to respondRecording how many problems the student answered correctly out of how many math problems total
DurationHow long the behavior occursRecording the minutes and seconds a student is out of the seat
RateFrequency of behavior in a specific amount of timeRecording how many times a student called out per minute during math class
LatencyThe time between the presentation of a stimulus and the occurrence of the behaviorRecording the time between asking a student to begin a test and when the student begins the test
Interval recordingEstimates the frequency of behavior using time sampling, partial interval, or whole interval recording
  • Time sampling: setting a timer for 10 minutes and recording whether the student was in seat when the interval ended
  • Partial interval: setting a timer for 5 minutes and recording whether the student left the seat at any time during the interval
  • Whole interval: setting a timer for 2 minutes and recording whether the student remained in seat the entire 2 minutes

Most often, teachers concern themselves with how often a response occurs (frequency) and the percentage of time it occurs during instructional activities (percentage correct). However, not all interventions focus on frequency of responding. Teachers may be interested in time spent in seat (duration), how many times per hour a student is out of seat (rate), or how quickly the student returns to the seat after a cue is given (latency). Collecting these data requires ongoing observation of the student, which may not always be feasible in large classrooms; therefore, data collection techniques that estimate behavior — time sampling and interval recording — can be used.

After appropriate data collection methods are identified, practitioners determine baseline levels of performance: the level of performance prior to intervention. This baseline represents the starting point against which practitioners compare a student's performance after beginning an intervention. Teachers examine baseline data to determine when a student will achieve the desired level of performance, usually by plotting four or five baseline data points on a line graph and drawing a trend line. The teacher later plots intervention data on the graph and draws a new trend line that illustrates current performance. If the objective is to increase a target behavior, the desired trend line slopes upward; if the goal is to decrease it, the desired trend line slopes downward. If intervention data indicate the skill will be mastered by the expected date, the intervention is considered effective and implementation continues. If the plotted points indicate the intervention is ineffective or has negative effects, then it is altered or discontinued and a different intervention is adopted. Phase change lines and condition labels indicate a condition change.

Recommendations for data collection

  1. Clearly define the target behavior.
  2. Select an appropriate data collection method.
  3. Establish the current level of responding by collecting baseline data.
  4. Set a schedule for collecting and reviewing data once instruction or intervention begins.
  5. Collect data and make changes to intervention as needed.

Punishment

The decreased use of aversive procedures can be attributed to developing effective function-based interventions; however, the shift away from aversive procedures also can be credited to the BACB establishing, enforcing, monitoring, and frequently updating an ethical compliance code for behavior analysts (BACB, 2017). Although the code does not apply to non-BCBA practitioners, it serves as a thorough document outlining BCBAs' conduct as well as their responsibility to their clients and the field. For instance, it outlines a client's right to effective treatment, which includes using interventions with proven short- and long-term benefits. Behavior analysts also are required to "recommend reinforcement rather than punishment whenever possible" (BACB, 2017, p. 13), and when punishment procedures are required, behavior analysts must include a reinforcement component for an alternative behavior.

Teachers also can follow the BACB's guidance by asking the following questions prior to considering the use of punitive or more restrictive procedures:

  1. Is there a behavior that can be taught or strengthened that will functionally replace the target behavior?
  2. Can the environment be adjusted so that the target behavior is no longer necessary?

For example, when attempting to decrease the number of times a student calls out during class, a teacher might teach the student to raise a hand as an alternative method to obtain the teacher's attention and ignore talk-outs instead of delivering derisive statements. Ultimately, interventions are designed to help students meet their needs by teaching and reinforcing functional skills.

Function-based assessments

Prior to designing behavior reduction interventions, an educational team should conduct a functional behavioral assessment (FBA). An FBA involves data collection methods aimed at evaluating environmental variables and their relation to the target behavior. These assessment methods use a person-centered approach and do not treat behaviors such as aggression (hitting, kicking, spitting) the same way for every student. Instead, practitioners determine why the person engages in aggression toward others and create function-based interventions to help students express their needs in more socially appropriate ways (Cooper et al., 2007).

For example, after thorough assessment, an intervention team might determine Walden hits his teacher when he is trying to escape a demand, but Eleanor hits her mother when she wants to play on the tablet. Instead of implementing the same procedures, or just a punishment-based program to decrease hitting, the team might help Walden learn to ask for a break and teach Eleanor to ask for a turn on the tablet.

Behavior analysts, teachers, paraprofessionals, students, and other stakeholders should all be key players of a collaborative team when conducting FBAs. Furthermore, it is important to not only understand the relation between the target behavior and environmental variables but to also understand the target behaviors' impact on those involved in these experiences, including the individual. Therefore, FBAs often involve indirect measures, such as parent or guardian questionnaires. The questionnaires primarily focus on events occurring immediately before and after the target behavior occurs, but they also often involve evaluating additional factors that might not be as obvious, such as sleep deprivation, medication changes, or changes in family structure.

Interview questions might include
  1. Where is the target behavior most and least likely to occur?
  2. With whom is the target behavior most and least likely to occur?
  3. What generally happens before, during, and after the target behavior occurs?
  4. How does the target behavior impact the individual's, and other family members', daily routines? For example, is the individual unable to attend certain events because of the target behavior?
  5. Describe the individual's morning, afternoon, and evening routines.

In addition to indirect assessments, an FBA also requires the behavior intervention team to conduct direct observations. For example, ABC (antecedent, behavior, consequence) data forms provide a format for practitioners to record events and other relevant information occurring before, during, and after the behavior occurs (Cooper et al., 2007). Eventually, this information might reveal a pattern leading to a hypothesis about the function of the target behavior. Direct observations might indicate that the target behavior occurs in a specific context or during a particular time of day, or more explicit pattern details might emerge, such as target behavior occurring after the individual is denied access to a preferred item.

Although antecedents are important to note, the consequence influences whether or not the behavior will continue to occur (Skinner, 1938). For example, after all teachers entered grades earlier than expected, a principal allowed teachers to wear jeans. If wearing jeans is a preferred activity for the teachers, the jeans-day provision will likely influence early grade entry in the future.

Events occurring after the target behavior can be classified into a few categories: access to an item or attention, escape from work or an undesired social situation, or automatic stimulation (Cooper et al., 2007). For example, after conducting direct observations, a data collector might note that during group instruction, Emily called out to answer a question, rather than raising her hand, eight out of nine times. This information, combined with indirect measures, might lead to a hypothesis indicating access to peer attention as a potential reason Emily engages in yelling behavior. Without identifying peer attention as the reason, the teacher might attempt ignoring Emily's yelling, which would likely have little to no effect due to the value of peer attention. However, after identifying the appropriate function, the team can develop interventions to reduce peer attention, such as a group behavior game rewarding students for ignoring inappropriate behavior (e.g., classwide function-related intervention teams; Kamps et al., 2011).

Group contingencies

TypeExample
Dependent
The entire group earns a reward based on the behavior of one or more group members (e.g., Jones, Boon, Fore, & Bender, 2008).
  1. Prior to the lesson, the teacher reviews and practices classroom rules; secretly selects a student; determines the number of observation checks; and determines an obtainable point goal (6 is not obtainable if the teacher plans to check five times).
  2. During the lesson, the teacher periodically observes the student during intervals, observes whether the student is following predetermined rules, and provides a point during observation checks if the student is following all rules.
  3. After the lesson, if the goal is met the teacher reveals the student and provides a reward to the entire class; if not, the teacher does not reveal the student and states the mystery student did not meet the goal.
Interdependent
The entire group earns a reward if every group member follows the rules (e.g., Kamps et al., 2011).		 The teacher follows the same steps as the dependent group contingency, except: groups are announced at the beginning of the game; during planned checks the teacher observes and records whether an entire group met the criteria rather than an individual; and groups receive rewards based on the group's performance rather than an individual's.
Independent
All individuals meeting the criteria receive a reward (e.g., Chazin & Ledford, 2016).		 The teacher tells the class, "Every student earning an A on the test will receive an extra homework pass," and then provides the homework pass to all students who scored an A.

Functional communication training

Practitioners implementing behavior-analytic techniques are most interested in increasing individuals' independence or freedom to choose, negotiate for, and access preferred items and activities within their environment, and limiting their exposure to aversive situations. This person-centered approach may be best exemplified in the application of functional communication training (FCT). Carr and Durand (1985) developed FCT to reduce challenging behavior while simultaneously honoring an individual's need to communicate. Instead of merely trying to reduce a target behavior, FCT asks practitioners to determine why the individual is engaging in the behavior and to then provide a conventional and likely more effective way to ask for assistance. FCT has been established as an evidence-based practice for use with individuals with developmental disabilities (Wong et al., 2015).

In general, FCT has five steps that must be followed to ensure success. First, the practitioner conducts an FBA to identify current reinforcers maintaining challenging behavior (e.g., access to attention, escape from tasks) through interviews, direct observation, and in some cases the manipulation of environmental variables. Second, the communicative response that serves the same function as the challenging behavior is identified — if an FBA indicates a student's challenging behavior is to escape difficult tasks, a teacher might have the student touch a card with the words "I want a break" as the alternate functional communicative response (FCR). It is important that this response require minimal effort and be understood by all available listeners: the goal is to make the FCR more effective than the challenging behavior in helping the student obtain a desired outcome.

Third, the teacher must take time to teach the FCR and make sure it rests firmly in the student's repertoire. This often requires directly teaching the response within environments similar to those in which the target behavior occurs. For example, when teaching a student to ask for a break, the teacher might present a difficult task and, when observing precursors to challenging behavior (stops working, talking under one's breath), prompt the student to ask for a break. Fourth, the teacher must apply differential reinforcement — respond immediately and consistently to the FCR and lessen the response or not respond at all to challenging behavior (Tiger, Hanley, & Bruzek, 2008). Finally, as it is not practical to provide continuous access to reinforcers, the teacher must systematically fade reinforcement; for instance, after the student masters asking for a break rather than engaging in the target behavior, the teacher might introduce a short waiting period.

StepDescription
1. Identify reinforcers that maintain challenging behavior
  • Use indirect assessment — teacher–guardian questionnaires
  • Use direct observations — ABC data
  • Identify potential reinforcers: escape from work or an uncomfortable social situation; access to an activity or item
2. Identify a functional communicative response (FCR)
  • AAC device, gesture, picture exchange, or vocalization indicating a desired break, attention, or item
  • A code system, such as a red-and-green card on the student's desk — red indicates a break request, green indicates attention or help
  • Raising one's hand instead of calling out
3. Teach the FCR
  • Teach in natural settings and when the motivation is present
  • Initially, the student might require prompting to acquire the FCR
  • Contrive situations to allow more opportunities to practice — if the student escapes work by tearing up worksheets, place the work on the desk and prompt the student to request a break
4. Apply differential reinforcement
  • Reinforce the FCR and weaken the response to problem behavior
  • Attention-maintained: if the student raises a hand instead of blurting out, the teacher praises and provides high-quality attention; if the student blurts out, the teacher provides limited or low-quality attention
  • Escape-maintained: if the student tears up spelling work to escape math, the teacher continues to provide math work and prompts through completion; if the student requests a break, the teacher removes the work and allows a break
5. Gradually fade reinforcement for the FCR
  • Monitor data to determine if, when and how to begin fading — but remember not all reinforcement schedules should be faded (a teacher's paycheck is not faded)
  • Rather than allowing a break every time, allow a break every other time or set a work requirement before a break is available
  • Rather than calling on students every time they raise a hand, call on them every other time, then every third time, and so on
  • Continuously monitor data to determine changes in the reinforcement schedule

Antecedent strategies

To enhance the effects of positive reinforcement, teachers might use antecedent strategies — strategies in place to reduce the likelihood of the target behavior occurring — which have the potential to decrease an individual's target behaviors while simultaneously increasing their use of contextually appropriate behaviors. Examples include the provision of choices, high-probability request sequences, and embedding demands into preferred activities (Miltenberger, 2006).

One specific example of an antecedent strategy is a "first-then" statement. A first-then statement specifies the contingency before a demand or task is placed and informs the student what needs to be done before the student can access reinforcement. For example, when the student is working independently on a math problem, a teacher might state, "First, do your math problems; then, you can use the computer." Similar models are used in everyday life for adults — when the school district provided Mr. Taylor with a contract, the district essentially made a first-then statement: "First, work; then, you get a paycheck."

Conclusion

At the end of their meeting, Dr. Thatcher said, "In your future special education practice, ABA can be used for teaching academic, social, and adaptive skills across different populations and settings. It can even be used to improve your own practice, to improve the practice of your paraprofessionals, and to make your entire school a more effective and welcoming place for students. If you decide to become a behavior-analytic practitioner, stay curious and continue to seek answers to your questions."

ABA — the application of behavioral principles to important social issues — has long served a crucial role in programming for special education students. Its procedures have been well established in the research literature and many deemed evidence-based practices for students with and without disabilities. Unfortunately, it is often subject to misconceptions (e.g., that it is only for students with ASD) and in some cases associated with a past marred by the use of unsavory practices (e.g., restraint, electric shock). However, the adoption of behavior-analytic practice can be an important tool for teachers. ABA complements any educational endeavor in that it places the learner front and center within intervention and instructional design and demands that educators are accountable and resilient in supporting students in achieving the highest quality of life possible.
