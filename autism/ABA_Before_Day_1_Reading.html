Chapter One

What Is Autism?

Autism is a life-long developmental disability that prevents people from understanding what they see, hear, and otherwise sense. This results in severe problems with social relationships, communication, and behaviour.

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, DSM-IV (American Psychiatric Association, 1994) defines autism as a pervasive developmental disorder characterized by:

  • impairments in communication and social interaction, and
  • restricted, repetitive, and stereotypic patterns of behaviour, interests, and activities.

It is a complex neurological disorder that affects the functioning of the brain.

Autism symptoms can be present in a variety of combinations and may accompany other disabilities. Some people with autism have normal levels of intelligence, while most people with autism have some level of intellectual disability, ranging from mild to severe. This range is often referred to as high-functioning autism to low-functioning autism.

There may be a range of difficulties in expressive and receptive language and communication. It is estimated that up to 50% of people with autism do not develop functional speech. For those who do, speech may have unusual qualities and have limited communicative functions.

All people with autism have difficulties with social interaction and behaviour, but the extent and type of difficulty may vary. Some individuals may be very withdrawn, while others may be overly active and approach people in peculiar ways. They have problems with inattention and resistance to change. They often respond to sensory stimuli in an atypical manner and may exhibit odd behaviours such as hand flapping, spinning, or rocking. They may also demonstrate unusual uses of objects and attachments to objects.

Key idea

Although people with autism share some common features, no two individuals are the same. The pattern and extent of difficulties may change with development. Combine general knowledge of autism with knowledge of the specific interests, abilities, and personality of each student.

Prevalence of autism

The generally accepted prevalence rate for autism has been between four and five in every 10,000 births. However, some recent estimates suggest a rate of about 10 in 10,000 (0.1% of children) or more, when a broader spectrum of disorders is included. There is a higher prevalence among males: studies reveal a male-to-female ratio of between 3:1 and 4:1.

Causes of autism

The cause or combination of causes of autism is not fully known. There is growing evidence that autism is a genetic condition, and that there are likely several different genes involved. The mode of genetic transmission appears complex, and scientists are focusing their work on finding which genes may be involved and how these genes are affected. So far, it appears that for at least a significant subgroup of persons with autism, there is a genetic susceptibility which differs across families (that is, different genes may be responsible in different families).

There is also evidence that there may be a higher prevalence among children with autism of problems very early in the mother's pregnancy, at birth, or even after birth than for children who do not have autism. Early life events and environmental factors may interact significantly with genetic susceptibility in the child.

Recently, various types of investigations, including imaging studies, electro-encephalographic studies, tissue studies on autopsy material, and neuro-chemical studies, have provided further evidence of a biological basis for autism. The brains of individuals with autism appear to have some structural and functional differences from the brains of other people. Anomalies in the brain stem and cranial nerves have been found. Ongoing research may one day pinpoint the exact genes and other conditions that combine to cause autism.

Diagnosis of autism

The diagnosis of autism is made by a physician or clinical psychologist with expertise in the area of autism. Assessment and diagnosis ideally should involve a multidisciplinary team that includes:

  • a pediatrician or psychiatrist — a medical assessment rules out other possible causes for the symptoms, as many characteristics of autism are also present in other disorders;
  • a psychologist — often administers assessments of developmental level and behaviour;
  • a speech and language pathologist — assesses speech, language, and communicative behaviours.

A medical and developmental history is taken through discussion with the parents. This information is combined with the other assessments to provide the overall picture and to rule out other contributing factors. Parents seeking additional information regarding assessment and diagnosis should contact health professionals in their community.

Professionals diagnose autism through the presence or absence of certain behaviours, characteristic symptoms, and developmental delays. The criteria are outlined in the DSM-IV and the International Classification of Diseases (World Health Organization, 1993).

The DSM-IV, most commonly used in North America, classifies autism within a broader group of pervasive developmental disorders (PDD) — an umbrella term for disorders that involve impairments in reciprocal social interaction skills, communication skills, and the presence of stereotypical behaviours, interests, and activities. The conditions classified as PDD in the DSM-IV are:

  • Autism
  • Childhood Disintegrative Disorder (CDD)
  • Rett's Disorder
  • Asperger's Disorder
  • Pervasive Developmental Disorder Not Otherwise Specified (PDD-NOS)

Some of these diagnostic terms appear to be used interchangeably within the literature and by practitioners. The term Autism Spectrum Disorders is sometimes used to refer to autism and other conditions within the PDD classification. PDD is sometimes used to refer to all conditions within the category, and at other times to PDD-NOS. This confusion of terminology can be a problem when people from different disciplines are working together to support students.

DSM-IV criteria for autistic disorder (299.00)

  1. A A total of at least six items from (1), (2), and (3), with at least two from (1), and one each from (2) and (3):
    1. (1) Qualitative impairment in social interaction, as shown by at least two of:

    2. (2) Qualitative impairments in communication, as shown by at least one of:

      2. In individuals with adequate speech, marked impairment in the ability to initiate or sustain a conversation with others

Other pervasive developmental disorders

All of the disorders within the PDD classification have some common features, and children with these disorders may benefit from similar instructional strategies. However, there are differences in some areas, such as the number of symptoms, age of onset, and developmental pattern.

In British Columbia, students who have developmental disorders but are not diagnosed as autistic may be identified for special education funding purposes in the category that best reflects their medical condition and the type and intensity of educational interventions required. In some cases, the appropriate category may be "Students With Intellectual Disabilities." In others, it may be "Severe Behaviour Disorders," "Moderate Behaviour Disorders," or "Physical Disabilities/Chronic Health Impairments." Some students may meet the ministry criteria for "Severe Learning Disabilities."

Asperger's syndrome

Asperger's syndrome shares many of the features of autism. People with Asperger's syndrome have disabilities in the areas of social interaction and stereotypical behaviour patterns.

The main difference is that children with AS do not have clinically significant delays in early language development or in cognitive development. They usually do not have the same degree of difficulty as those with autism in the development of age-appropriate self-help skills, adaptive behaviour, and curiosity about the environment in childhood.

The DSM-IV uses the term Asperger's Disorder; this guide uses Asperger's syndrome, consistent with current literature. See Chapter 6 for more.

Chapter Two

Characteristics Associated with Autism

Although every person with autism is unique, some characteristics are considered particularly important in the diagnosis. These fall into four major categories:

  • communication
  • social interaction
  • unusual behaviour
  • learning

Other characteristics of behaviour and learning can be categorized as:

  • unusual patterns of attention
  • unusual responses to sensory stimuli
  • anxiety

This chapter addresses these seven categories and outlines instructional implications for each.

1. Communication

All people with autism experience language and communication difficulties, although there are considerable differences in ability. Some are non-verbal while others may have extensive language with deficits in pragmatics (the social use of language). People with autism may seem caught up in a private world in which communication is unimportant. This is not an intentional action but rather an inability to communicate.

Language difficulties that may be present include:

  • Non-verbal communication difficulties: inappropriate facial expressions, unusual use of gestures, lack of eye contact, strange body postures, lack of mutual or shared focus of attention
  • Delay in or lack of expressive language skills
  • Differences in oral language (for those who develop it): odd pitch or intonation; faster or slower rate than normal; unusual rhythm or stress; monotone or lilting voice quality
  • Repetitive and idiosyncratic speech patterns
  • Echolalia — immediate or delayed literal repetition of the speech of others. It:
    • appears to be non-meaningful, but may indicate an attempt to communicate
    • indicates the ability to produce speech and to imitate
    • may serve a communication or cognitive purpose for the student
  • Restricted vocabulary: dominated by nouns; often confined to requests or rejections to regulate one's physical environment; limited in social functions
  • Perseveration — a tendency to continually discuss one topic and have difficulty changing topics
  • Difficulty with the pragmatics of conversation: problems initiating; difficulty using unwritten rules; inability to maintain conversation on a topic; inappropriate interrupting; inflexible, stereotypic style of speaking

"The student may be using echolalic utterances to rehearse what is heard in order to process the information, or as a strategy for self-regulation."

— B. Prizant & J. Duchan, "The functions of immediate echolalia in autistic children," 1981

People with autism often have difficulty comprehending verbal information, following long verbal instructions, and remembering a sequence of instructions. Comprehension may be context-specific. Even those with normal intelligence (usually called high-functioning) may have difficulty with comprehension of verbal information.

Implications for instruction

Effective programs include comprehensive communication assessment and intervention — typically assessment by a speech and language pathologist plus informal observation and classroom-based evaluation. This forms the basis for goals, objectives, and strategies for receptive and expressive language, particularly pragmatic skills. Instruction should emphasize paying attention, imitating, comprehending, and using language in play and social interaction. Communication goals should emphasize the functional use of language in various settings.

2. Social interaction

Students with autism demonstrate qualitative differences in social interaction and often have difficulty establishing relationships. They may have limited social interactions or a rigid way of interacting with others. These difficulties should not be seen as a lack of interest or unwillingness to interact; they may result from an inability to distill social information from the interaction and use appropriate communication skills to respond.

"One must separate the variables of social interaction problems from emotions. People with autism desire emotional contact with other people but they are stymied by complex social interaction."

— Temple Grandin, Thinking in Pictures, 1995, p. 44

Understanding social situations requires language processing and non-verbal communication, which are often areas of deficit. People with autism may not notice important social cues, and may miss necessary information. They typically have impairment in using non-verbal behaviours and gestures to regulate social interaction, and may have difficulty reading the non-verbal behaviour of others.

They have significant difficulty with any interaction that requires knowledge of other people and what they think or know. Baron-Cohen has described this as a deficit in theory of mind: people with autism may not be able to understand the perspective of others, or even that other people have a perspective different from their own. They may also have difficulty understanding their own — and particularly others' — beliefs, desires, intentions, knowledge, and perceptions. For example, a child with autism may not understand that another child is sad, even if that child is crying, because they are not themselves sad.

Students with autism show these difficulties in observable ways: playing with toys and objects in unusual and stereotypical ways; excessive or inappropriate laughing or giggling; play that lacks imaginative, social qualities; playing near others without sharing or taking turns; or withdrawing entirely from social situations.

Social interaction occurs on a continuum, with three subtypes:

SubtypeDescription
AloofNo observable interest in interacting with others except to satisfy basic needs; may become agitated in close proximity to others and reject unsolicited physical or social contact.
PassiveDoes not initiate social approaches, but will accept initiations from others.
Active but oddApproaches others for social interaction, but in an unusual and often inappropriate fashion.

People with autism do not necessarily fall into one distinct spot on the continuum.

Implications for instruction

Social skill development is essential and is an important component in plans for managing challenging behaviours. Students with autism do not learn social skills incidentally by observation and participation. It is generally necessary to target specific skills for explicit instruction and to provide support for using them in social situations. (See Strategies for teaching social skills.)

3. Unusual behaviour

People with autism often demonstrate unusual and distinctive behaviours, including:

  • restricted range of interests, and preoccupation with one specific interest or object
  • inflexible adherence to a non-functional routine
  • stereotypic and repetitive motor mannerisms — hand flapping, finger flicking, rocking, spinning, walking on tiptoes, spinning objects
  • preoccupation with parts of objects
  • fascination with movement, such as a spinning fan or turning wheels on toys
  • insistence on sameness and resistance to change
  • unusual responses to sensory stimuli
Implications for instruction

Many of these behaviours may be caused by other factors: hyper- or hypo-sensitivity to sensory stimulation, difficulty understanding social situations, difficulty with changes in routine, and anxiety. The plan should include strategies for expanding interests, developing skills, understanding sensory responses, and preparing the student for planned changes.

Consider the problem behaviour and its function for that child — for example, gaining attention or avoiding something. It may not be possible to eliminate all repetitive behaviours. Successful strategies often focus on environmental adaptations to decrease an inappropriate behaviour, and/or teaching a more appropriate behaviour that serves the same function.

4. Learning

People with autism have a psycho-educational profile different from typically developing individuals. There may be deficits in many cognitive functions, yet not all are affected; complex abilities may be impaired while simpler abilities in the same area are intact. Research identifies these cognitive features:

  • deficits in paying attention to relevant cues and information, and in attending to multiple cues
  • receptive and expressive language impairments, particularly using language to express abstract concepts
  • deficits in concept formation and abstract reasoning
  • impairment in social cognition — sharing attention and emotion with others, and understanding others' feelings
  • inability to plan, organize, and solve problems

Some students have stronger abilities in rote memory and visual-spatial tasks. They may excel at puzzles and perform well at spatial, perceptual, and matching tasks. Some may recall simple information but have difficulty recalling more complex information.

"When I was a child and a teenager, I thought everybody thought in pictures. I had no idea that my thought processes were different."

— Temple Grandin, Thinking in Pictures, 1995, p. 20

Temple Grandin suggests that some people with autism more easily learn and remember information presented visually, and may have problems learning about things that cannot be thought about in pictures.

Students may have difficulty comprehending oral and written information — following directions or understanding what they read — yet some higher-functioning individuals may identify words, apply phonetic skills, and know word meanings. Some show strength in sound production (phonology), vocabulary, and simple grammar (syntax), yet struggle with conversation and social use of speech (pragmatics). A high-functioning student may compute numbers easily yet be unable to solve math problems.

Implications for instruction

Cognitive development is usually uneven. Base the education program on the individual's unique combination of strengths and needs. Given documented deficits in attention, language, concept formation, and memory for complex information — plus personal accounts of visual orientation — incorporate visual material when teaching.

5. Unusual patterns of attention

Students often have difficulty paying attention to relevant cues and may focus only on a restricted part of the environment. For example, a student may look at the ball but not at the person to whom it is to be thrown, or notice a staple in the corner of a paper but not the information on it. This is called stimulus over-selectivity. Another feature is an impaired capacity to share attention between two things or people — joint attention.

Students may also have difficulty disengaging and shifting attention from one stimulus to the next, which may contribute to rigidity and resistance to change. They may also have a short attention span.

Implications for instruction

Attention difficulties may affect social behaviour and language — e.g., responding to irrelevant cues, or attending to only part of a conversation. Present information in a format that is clear, focuses attention, and emphasizes the most relevant information. Develop individualized strategies for focusing attention; parents can share methods that work at home. Ideally, the student eventually learns to manage these strategies independently.

6. Unusual responses to sensory stimuli

Responses to sensory stimulation may range from hyposensitivity (under-reactive) to hypersensitivity (over-reactive). Environmental stimuli may be disturbing or even painful to someone with autism, for any type of sensory input. Other characteristics associated with autism may be caused, in part, by a disorder in sensory processing — so consider both the type and amount of stimulation in the environment and the individual's reaction to it.

Tactile system

The skin gathers information through touch, temperature, and pressure, interpreted as pain, neutral information, or pleasure. Students affected in this system may withdraw when touched (tactile defensive), or overreact to textures of objects, clothing, or food — which can lead to behaviour problems, irritability, or withdrawal. Some types and amounts of stimulation may instead have a calming effect.

Auditory system

Students may be hypo- or hypersensitive to sounds. Seemingly innocuous sounds can cause extreme responses — a problem in schools full of chair scraping, bells, intercom announcements, and machinery. People with autism report such sounds can seem excruciatingly intense.

Visual and olfactory systems

Some students react to odours such as perfumes and deodorants; others use smell to explore their surroundings in unexpected ways. Some cover their eyes to avoid certain lighting, reflections, or shiny objects, while others seek out shiny things and look at them for long periods.

"As I walk down the street, I know what everyone is having for dinner by the smells coming from the houses."

— A 14-year-old boy with Asperger's syndrome

Vestibular and proprioceptive systems

The inner ear detects movement and changes in position. Students may be fearful of movement and have trouble on stairs or ramps, seeming strangely fearful or clumsy — or the opposite, actively seeking intense movement like whirling and spinning. Students who have trouble integrating information from muscles and body parts may have odd posture and appear clumsy.

Implications for instruction

Aversive sensory experiences may contribute to inappropriate behaviours. People with severe sensory processing problems may shut down to avoid over-stimulation; tantrums may be related to escaping over-stimulating situations; self-stimulating behaviours may help the individual calm down by generating a self-controlled, repetitive stimulus. Teachers and families can work together to assess sensory responses, and use strategies to enhance responses when a student is hypo-responsive and calm them when hyper-responsive.

7. Anxiety

Anxiety is not in the DSM-IV criteria, but many people with autism, their parents, and teachers identify it as a characteristic. Sources may include:

  • inability to express oneself
  • difficulties processing sensory information
  • fearing some sources of sensory stimulation
  • a high need for predictability, and difficulty with change
  • difficulty understanding social expectations
  • fearing situations because they are not understood
Implications for instruction

Programs often need to address anxiety and what contributes to it. Adapt the environment to reduce anxiety-arousing situations, and use a variety of strategies to help the individual manage anxiety and cope with difficult situations.

Chapter Four

Teaching Students with Autism

No single method for teaching students with autism is successful for all students, and students' needs change over time. This chapter covers approaches that have proved successful, in five sections: instructional approaches, classroom management, communication development, social skills, and functional skills.

Instructional approaches

Visual approaches

The most strongly recommended approach is to use visual aids. Students often show relative strengths in concrete thinking, rote memory, and visual-spatial understanding, and difficulties in abstract thinking, social cognition, communication, and attention. Pictographic and written cues can help the student learn, communicate, and develop self-control.

Visual aids stay available for as long as the student needs to process the information. Oral information is transient — once said, it's gone — which is a problem for students who process language slowly or struggle to block out background stimulation.

Visual aids range from concrete to abstract:

Real object / situation→Facsimile→Colour photo→Colour picture→B&W picture→Line drawing→Graphic symbol→Written language
ConcreteAbstract

Graphic symbols, though abstract, have been widely successful. To work, visual supports must match the student's level of comprehension — using a line drawing when the student needs colour photographs will only frustrate everyone. Visual supports can be used to:

  • organize activity — daily schedules, mini-schedules, activity checklists, calendars, choice boards
  • give directions — visual display of assignments, file cards with task directions, pictographs and written instructions
  • clarify the environment — labels on objects and containers, signs, lists, charts, messages
  • support appropriate behaviour — posted rules, visual steps of routines
  • teach social skills — illustrated social stories for specific situations
  • teach self-control — pictographs that cue behaviour expectations
The key question

When planning an activity or giving an instruction, ask: how can this information be presented in a simple visual format? Choose visual aids based on an understanding of the student and their abilities and responses.

Provide precise, positive praise while the student is learning

Tell students exactly what they did right: "great colouring," "good finishing of that math problem." Students with autism may learn in one trial, so direct praise to the specific behaviour: "Sal, you are doing very well at multiplying these numbers." Generalized praise risks superstitious learning — if you say "Sal, you are doing very well" while Sal is also swinging his feet, he may connect the foot swinging with the praise.

Use meaningful reinforcements

Reinforcers can be anything from praise to tangible objects that increase the target behaviour. Students with autism may not be motivated by common reinforcers. They might prefer time alone, talking to a preferred staff member, a trip to the cafeteria, a walk, time with a desired object, music, water play, a favourite routine, specific sensory items, or sitting at the window. Build a "likes and dislikes" preference profile with the family and share it with all service providers.

Plan tasks at an appropriate level of difficulty

Students may be especially vulnerable to anxiety and frustration when they can't perform a task. Increase difficulty gradually and scaffold learning — particularly with visual information rather than oral explanation alone.

Use age-appropriate materials

Honour the student's dignity: even if instruction is heavily modified, materials should suit the student's age.

Provide opportunities for choice

Much of these students' lives may be highly structured by adults, and some keep choosing one thing because they don't know how to choose another. Develop individual methods of offering choice and teach choosing directly. Limit choice to one or two preferred activities until the student grasps the concept — open-ended choices only frustrate.

Break down oral instructions into small steps

Avoid long strings of verbal information; support oral instruction with visual cues.

Pay attention to processing and pacing

Students may need longer to respond, due to cognitive and/or motor difficulties, and may need to process each piece of a message separately. Allow extra time generally, and ample wait time between instruction and response.

Use concrete examples and hands-on activities

Teach abstract ideas with specific examples, and vary the examples so the concept isn't accidentally learned as applying in only one way.

Use task analysis

Break complex tasks into sub-tasks and reinforce small, teachable steps. For example, brushing teeth becomes: get toothbrush and toothpaste → turn on water → wet toothbrush → unscrew toothpaste lid → put toothpaste on brush → … Life, social, and academic skills can all be taught this way, each step taught and then linked to the next in a chain.

Use discrete trial methods

Prompts may be physical, gestural, or verbal, and should be used only as long as needed — students can become dependent on them. In a discrete trial, the instructor presents the stimulus (instruction), gives a prompt, the student responds, and the instructor provides a consequence based on behavioural principles.

Example objective: Given an oral direction, Jackie will touch the card with the correct number when presented with cards numbered 1–5.

Introduce unfamiliar tasks in a familiar environment when possible

When that isn't possible, prepare the student for the new task and setting with pictures, videos, and/or social stories.

Organize materials to highlight what is important

Use organization aids and visual supports to help the student attend to pertinent information and learn new tasks. Remove extraneous materials from the desk before teaching; present only the text to be read rather than the whole book; highlight key words such as character names.

Encourage independence and avoid prompt dependency

Constantly supported students may never learn to act independently. Strategies include:

  • using visual aids to reduce reliance on physical and verbal prompts
  • planning ways to fade prompts
  • not always positioning the adult close to the student, and varying which adults are present (e.g., rotating teacher assistants)
  • providing visual organizers — schedules, task outlines, checklists, charts — and involving the student in making them
  • teaching awareness of environmental cues
  • teaching in the environments containing the cues and reinforcement that will prompt and maintain the behaviour

Direct and broaden fixations into useful activities

If the student is fixated on an object or topic (a colour, a shape), use it to teach concepts. A whole week of writing and math can centre on one topic.

Know the individual

Keep a list of strengths and interests. Families can tell you what the student knows and does at home and in the community — build on these for instruction and reinforcement.

Develop talent and interest areas

If the student shows strength in an area (music, drama, art, graphics, computers), provide opportunities to develop expertise. This brings enjoyment and success, and may lead to future employment skills.

Strategies for classroom management

Provide a structured, predictable environment

This is not an authoritarian approach. Structure provides consistency and clarity so students know where things belong, what is expected, and what comes next.

Provide a customized visual daily schedule

The student's schedule should fit within the class schedule. Vary tasks to prevent boredom; alternate familiar, successful activities with less preferred ones; alternate large-group activities with quiet calming time; build in physical activity throughout the day. Post the schedule at or near the student's desk. Teach the student to use it independently, and direct them to it at transition times.

  • over-reacts to smells in environment
  • smell-defensive — avoids places or people with strong odours
  • seems to constantly want food
  • licks objects in the environment
  • chews on objects inappropriately
  • high threshold for bad tastes — dangerous substances not avoided
  • sniffs objects and people in unusual ways
  • does not notice smells others notice

"Autism cuts me off from my own body, so I feel nothing. It can also make me so aware of what I feel that it is painful."

— Donna Williams, an adult with autism, Somebody Somewhere, 1994

Building a sensory inventory

An inventory of sensory factors helps minimize their negative effect. Parents and others who know the student are valuable sources. Questions to consider:

Auditory

  • Fans, loudspeakers, fire alarms, several people talking, air conditioners, bells, barking, scraping?
  • General sound level; predictability and repetitiveness of sounds?
  • What can minimize their effect?
  • Student's verbal comprehension and time needed to process and shift auditory attention.

Visual

  • Distracters — light, movement, reflection, background patterns?
  • Student's eye level; teacher's position relative to student.
  • Time required to shift visual attention.
  • Reducing aversive visual stimuli helps behaviour and learning.

Tactile

  • Aversive textures?
  • Appropriate temperatures?
  • Does the student need to explore through touch, yet avoid being touched?
  • Ability or defensiveness with instructional objects.

Vestibular

  • The student's need to move and exercise.
  • Reactions to movement.
  • How to build in movement without disrupting other students.

Gustatory & olfactory

  • Taste and smell preferences for foods and materials.
  • Responses to smells of materials when choosing activities.
  • Effects on teaching snack/mealtime behaviour.

Note aspects of tasks that create frustration

Look for problem areas that may cause sensory overload or frustration. Offer calming sensory experiences alongside potentially frustrating tasks. Adapt tasks and materials to promote success, and reduce environmental distracters and confusing or upsetting activities where feasible.

Provide relaxation opportunities and areas

A calm, quiet, designated area may be needed. For some students, relaxing means engaging in calming repetitive behaviours; students who crave movements like rocking can be given a time and place where this is permitted.

Provide meaningful contact with peers who model appropriate behaviour

Teach appropriate social behaviour and situation-specific expectations. Opportunities include:

  • shared learning arrangements
  • buddies for hallways, playground, and unstructured times
  • varying buddies across time and activities to prevent dependence on one child
  • involving peers in individualized instruction
  • cross-age buddies (an older student assists)
  • pairing students at assemblies, clubs, and special events
  • facilitating after-school and extracurricular involvement

Plan for transitions and prepare the student for change

Changes in activity, setting, or routine are often very stressful. Visual schedules help students understand and cooperate with changes; illustrated social stories prepare them for new situations.

Strategies for communication development

Expanding communication is one of the greatest challenges for teachers and families. Most children develop communication automatically by age three or four; many students with autism have not and must be taught. Helping them express wants and needs, interact socially, share information, express emotions, and protest or escape aversive situations is a priority.

Instruction may begin in structured settings, but generalization requires natural settings — functional language is best taught where it will be used. Opportunity alone isn't enough, though: target specific skills and strategies. The school team, parents, and specialists (especially the speech and language pathologist) should collaborate on goals.

General suggestions:

  • Focus on interaction and communication in the environments where the child participates (classroom, playground, gym).
  • Use sentences — you're modelling speech as well as communicating.
  • Use vocabulary matched to comprehension; for more severe disability, choose familiar, specific, concrete words and repeat as needed.
  • Use clear, simple, concise language. Figures of speech, irony, and sarcasm confuse.
  • Allow processing time — talk more slowly or pause between words as needed.

"Autism…stops me from finding and using my own words when I want to. Or makes me use all the words and silly things I do not want to say."

— Donna Williams, Somebody Somewhere, 1994

Learning to listen

Students often need structured lessons on listening. Break listening into components — face the speaker, look at one spot (not necessarily eye contact), place hands in a planned position — and praise or reward each step.

Developing oral language comprehension

Pair spoken language with objects, pictures, and photographs to gain attention and aid comprehension. Interestingly, many students with autism use reading to support oral comprehension rather than the reverse, making reading instruction especially significant. With higher-functioning students, don't assume understanding just because they can repeat information — check for comprehension.

Developing oral language expression

Most students develop some form of communication, even if not traditional speech. Know the student's form of expression and adjust expectations; for students with limited speech, accept limited verbal attempts and non-verbal behaviour as communicative. A customized communication dictionary documents what the student says, what it means, and planned adult responses.

Teach new vocabulary in varied contexts with a visual approach. Students need to learn that:

  • everything in our world has a name
  • there are different ways of saying the same thing
  • words can be meaningful in a variety of contexts
  • using words helps them communicate their needs and desires

Students who use pictures need to learn that a picture has a name and can give direction. Include situations that encourage:

  • requests — for food, toys, or help
  • negation — refusing, protesting, or indicating they want to stop
  • commenting — labelling pictures or objects, greetings, play

Developing conversation skills

Virtually all people with autism struggle with pragmatics — even those with good vocabulary. Provide structured play built around the student's interests, using modelling, physical prompts, visual cues, and reinforcement. Simple drawings (e.g., Comic Strip Conversations) can show what people say, do, and think, with symbols for listening, interrupting, loud and quiet words, talk and thoughts; colours can represent emotions. Scripts help with specific situations. Give concrete rules in a visual format, and provide real social and community experiences to practise.

Echolalia

Echolalia — literal repetition of others' words — is part of normal early language development, but some learners with autism seem to stop developing at this level. It can be immediate or delayed (even months or years later).

Immediate echolalia can be a teaching tool: when a student echoes back a question, model the appropriate response and reinforce it when the student echoes that. Consult the SLP for individual strategies.

Delayed echolalia (e.g., repeating TV commercials word for word) can be thought of as a stored chunk of language triggered by a situation or emotion. Don't assume the student understands its content. Try to identify the trigger and prompt appropriate language — e.g., a student reciting a soft-drink ad turned out to be thirsty; the teacher tested this with "You feel thirsty and want a drink?" Sometimes no logical connection is ever found.

Alternative or augmentative communication (AAC)

An augmentative system is any approach that supports, enhances, or adds to how a person communicates — for non-verbal students or those who can't use speech functionally. Systems range from low-tech (no power) to high-tech. Alternative communication may include:

  • direct movement of a person or object (pulling a teacher to the door to go outside)
  • gestures or body actions (shaking the head)
  • real objects (bringing a jacket to ask to go home)
  • picture representations (e.g., the Picture Exchange Communication System, PECS)
  • voice without conventional words ("Ah-ah-ah" for the toilet)
  • written messages, pointed to or typed
  • sign language (e.g., ASL or Signed English)

Choosing a system should be based on assessment of cognitive ability, skills, interests, and motor abilities. The options form a continuum:

Full-sized objects→Miniature objects→Gestures→Photographs→Drawings→Written words→Written phrases→Written sentences→Signing
ConcreteHighly abstract

Parents are key: the system must be used at home and school to be effective. The SLP or another AAC specialist can help decide; the teacher often implements it. Computer-based communication and learning show promising results.

Strategies for teaching social skills

Most students with autism want to be part of the social world, but haven't automatically learned the unwritten rules. Many operate on rigid, overly literal misperceptions:

  • rules apply in only a single situation
  • everything someone says must be true
  • when you don't know what to do, do nothing

Never assume a student with autism understands a situation or social expectation. They may use an ineffective method because they don't know a better one, or can't distinguish between situations. Assess social competencies to determine which skills to teach directly. Integrated environments provide peer models and natural cues, but access alone is usually not enough — explicit teaching is needed.

Using social stories

Developed by Carol Gray, a social story describes a social situation — including social cues and appropriate responses — written for a specific situation and student. Uses include:

  • facilitating inclusion in regular classes
  • introducing changes and new routines
  • explaining others' behaviour
  • teaching situation-specific social skills
  • teaching new academic skills

A social story should describe the situation from the student's perspective, direct the appropriate behaviour, and be written in the student's voice ("I"). The author observes the situation, considers what the student sees, hears, and feels, then writes at the right comprehension level using three sentence types:

  • Descriptive — the setting, activity, and people involved
  • Directive — positive statements of the desired response
  • Perspective — possible reactions of others

Rule of thumb:

2–5 descriptive statements+1 directive statement=social story

Best format: a booklet with one or two sentences per page and one main concept per page.

Example: My Turn on the Computer

  1. p.1If I wait for my turn on the computer, the other kids like me better.
  2. p.2Everyone likes to have a turn on the computer.
  3. p.3When other kids are using the computer, I will be quiet and wait for my turn.
  4. p.4When I am finished on the computer, other kids can use it. That is okay, because I know I can use it the next day.
  5. p.5When I wait for my turn on the computer, everyone will be happy.

Three ways to implement a social story:

  • Independent reader: an adult reads it twice, the student reads it back, then reads it daily.
  • Non-reader: record it with a signal (e.g., a bell) to turn pages; the student "reads" it daily. Add symbols, drawings, or photos.
  • Modelling: videotape the story read aloud, one page on screen at a time.

Teaching key social rules

Understanding basic rules for a situation helps the child adapt, may reduce anxiety, and reduces reliance on inappropriate coping behaviours. Critical skills that usually need direct instruction:

SkillHow to teach it
WaitingVisual cues (object, pictures, written words) make waiting less abstract and more situation-specific.
Taking turnsSocial stories, a picture/pictograph cue, plus instruction and rehearsal in turn-taking activities.
TransitionsSocial stories and warnings with visual cues. Prepare the student for possibly finishing an activity later.
Changing the topicFor perseveration: visual rules, time limits, and a set time and place for the favourite topic.
FinishingTeach use of environmental cues (watch what other children do); use a timer and a self-check method.
InitiatingSocial stories with photos/pictures to teach approaching others, asking, joining a game, saying hello, and leaving if upset.
Being flexibleShow changes concretely — remove or cross out a picture in the schedule and put another in its place.
Being quietVisual supports for specific quiet behaviours and rules for specific situations.

Using Cognitive Picture Rehearsal

Present a sequence of behaviours as pictures or pictographs with a script, and guide the student through repeated practice of the sequence.

Using peer support

Educate peers first so they understand the student's behaviour — interpret non-verbal communication, explain what's hard, and show how to help. Young children can learn prompts to start and maintain interaction. Reinforce peers for their role too.

Pivotal Response Training (PRT), used at recess, has increased interaction, initiation, varied toy play, and language use. PRT teaches typical peers to:

  • gain attention
  • give choices to maintain motivation
  • vary toys
  • model social behaviour
  • reinforce attempts
  • encourage conversation
  • extend conversation
  • take turns
  • narrate play

Parents should be involved in any decision to discuss autism with their child's peers; they may wish to preview materials or help present.

Using social skills training groups

Small, structured groups can help. Good programs start with an assessment, and lessons follow a common format:

  1. Identify the skill, its components, and when it's used
  2. Model the skill
  3. Role play
  4. Practise
  5. Use strategies for generalization

These curricula weren't designed for autism, so adapt them and put extra emphasis on generalization.

Integrated play groups

For younger students, play groups with age peers create natural situations to express wants, practise being near other children, and imitate social interactions.

Teaching self-monitoring / self-management

For higher-functioning students, self-management — monitoring one's own behaviour to earn reinforcement — increases independence. Simply collecting self-monitoring data tends to increase the desired behaviour; accuracy matters less than the awareness it builds.

  1. Define the target behaviour the student will self-monitor.
  2. Identify reinforcers that work for the individual.
  3. Create a self-monitoring method (a chart, stickers, a low-tech counter).
  4. Teach the target behaviour and how to record it.
  5. Gradually reduce adult intervention so the student self-manages.

Supporting the development of friendships

Students with basic social skills may still struggle to connect with and maintain relationships with peers. Teachers and parents can help by:

  • encouraging a friend to play with the child at home
  • helping the student join school clubs, with support
  • teaching the child to observe other children to know what to do
  • encouraging co-operative games
  • modelling how to relate to the child and educating classmates
  • encouraging prospective friendships
  • making break times enjoyable
  • projects that illustrate the qualities of a good friend
  • directly teaching how to read faces and body language and respond to emotional cues

Teaching functional skills

A fundamental goal of schooling is independent functioning — especially important for students with autism. Functional skill goals should be in the student's IEP, taught with the same strategies used elsewhere. Functional skills span five domains:

Self-care

Hygiene, grooming, dressing, toileting (often needs significant planning), meal planning and food prep, eating, laundry, cleaning, handling money and budgeting (older students).

Functional academics

Communicating personal info (name, birth date, address, phone); reading signs and labels; measurement, counting, calendars, telling time.

Vocational

  • punctuality and reliable attendance
  • following a job routine; completing duties
  • understanding task completion
  • following safety procedures
  • accepting direction and correction
  • responding appropriately to authority
  • completing a cleanup routine
  • appropriate work attire and grooming
  • using breaks and lunch appropriately

Leisure

Team and individual sports, arts, performances, nature activities, organized groups, social events. At home: using TV/stereo, caring for pets, card games, crafts. Participation may be full or partial.

Community

Safety is a major concern. Consider: public transit; finding pools, rec centres, banks; pedestrian rules and traffic; public washrooms; restaurant skills like ordering food.

Schools and families should coordinate so instruction is consistent. Some skills involve very personal areas of life, so plan with sensitivity and care.

Chapter Six

Students with Asperger's Syndrome

Asperger's syndrome (AS) shares some characteristics with higher-functioning autism, but also has unique features and a different developmental progression and prognosis.

In British Columbia, supplemental funding designation for students with a medical diagnosis of AS should be based on the nature and extent of the disabling condition and the interventions required. These students may be reported in High Incidence categories such as "Moderate Behaviour Disorders" or "Severe Learning Disabilities," or, in a very few cases, "Severe Behaviour" or a Low Incidence category.

Under DSM-IV, a child diagnosed with AS must meet the criteria for social impairment, repetitive activities, and age of onset, but have normal cognition and early language development. AS involves fewer symptoms than autism and is considered a mild to moderate disability, while autism is usually considered severe.

Learning and behavioural characteristics

Social interaction

People with AS may be keen to relate to others but lack the skills, and may approach others in peculiar ways. They often lack understanding of social customs, appear socially awkward, have difficulty with empathy, and misinterpret social cues. Like people with autism, they may struggle to understand that others have their own perceptions, thoughts, and feelings. They are poor incidental social learners and need explicit instruction.

Language

Children with AS usually speak fluently by Kindergarten, but often have trouble with:

  • pragmatics — the use of language in social contexts
  • semantics — multiple meanings
  • prosody — pitch, stress, and rhythm of speech

They may have an advanced vocabulary and talk incessantly about a narrowly defined favourite subject, with difficulty switching topics. Because they don't naturally learn conversational rules, they may:

  • interrupt or talk over others
  • make irrelevant comments
  • have difficulty starting and ending conversations
  • speak with little variation in pitch, stress, and rhythm
  • use overly pedantic or formal speech, especially in adolescence
  • stand too close when talking
  • stare, or use unusual posture or body language
  • fail to understand others' gestures and facial expressions

Cognition and academics

Students with AS are of average to above-average intelligence and may appear very capable, often with extensive factual knowledge about an absorbing subject. But relative weaknesses in comprehension, abstract thought, and social cognition cause problems with:

  • reading comprehension
  • problem solving
  • organizational skills
  • concept development
  • making inferences and judgements

Thinking also tends to be rigid; they have difficulty adapting to change or failure and do not readily learn from mistakes.

Motor co-ordination

An estimated 50–90% of people with AS have motor co-ordination problems — locomotion, balance, manual dexterity, handwriting, rapid movements, rhythm, and imitating movements.

Sensory, attention, and anxiety

Students with AS may share autism's sensory differences — hypersensitivity to some stimuli, and unusual or repetitive behaviours to obtain specific sensory input. Many are inattentive and easily distracted, and some have been diagnosed with ADHD at some point. Anxiety is also common; understanding and adapting to school's social demands can be hard, and appropriate instruction and support can relieve some of the stress.
