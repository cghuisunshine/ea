How to use this tutorial

The goal is not to memorize isolated definitions. The goal is to learn a decision process you can apply while another person is distressed.

Observe behavior→Look for causes→Assess risk→Match your response→Reassess→Recover & learn
Important: This page is an independent study aid, not an official CPI course, certification, or substitute for employer policy. It intentionally does not teach physical disengagement or restraint maneuvers. Those require authorized hands-on instruction, competency assessment, local policy, and legal/professional oversight.

Three study passes

Pass 1 — Recognition

Learn the vocabulary and identify the behavior level.

Pass 2 — Response

For every example, decide what staff should do and what they should avoid.

Pass 3 — Reasoning

Explain why the response fits the risk and the person’s current level.

The big picture: prevention before reaction

The official outline describes a framework for preventing, de-escalating, and safely responding to crisis behavior. It emphasizes understanding distress, assessing behavioral risk, managing your own emotional response, using supportive and directive communication, and choosing the least restrictive response appropriate to the actual risk.

Core philosophy translated into everyday behavior

PrincipleWhat it looks like in practiceWhat it is not
CareProtect dignity, communicate respectfully, seek the reason behind distress.Rewarding unsafe behavior or removing all expectations.
WelfareConsider emotional, physical, sensory, medical, developmental, and environmental needs.Focusing only on rule compliance.
SafetyNotice hazards, reduce stimulation, create space, call appropriate support early.Waiting until someone is already being hurt.
SecurityUse predictable boundaries, plans, roles, and organizational procedures.Using control for its own sake.
Master question: “What response is most likely to reduce risk while preserving dignity and using no more restriction than the situation actually requires?”
Module 1

Understanding Behavior and Its Risk

Before deciding what to do, separate three things: what you observe, what might be causing it, and how much risk it actually presents.

1. Behavior is observable; causes are hypotheses

Observation

“Jordan is pacing, breathing quickly, checking the door, and asking when class ends every two minutes.”

Possible causes

Fear, sensory overload, uncertainty, pain, previous trauma, hunger, worry about transportation, difficulty with the task.

Notice that the cause is not stated as fact until you have evidence. Saying “Jordan is manipulating us” is an interpretation, not an observation.

2. Threat responses can narrow thinking

When a person perceives threat, their ability to process complex language, delay gratification, consider multiple options, and regulate emotion may decrease. In practice, that means a long lecture can become less effective exactly when you feel most tempted to give one.

Practical translation: As distress rises, simplify. Use fewer words, more processing time, a calmer tone, and clearer choices.

3. Risk = likelihood + severity

CPI’s Decision-Making Matrix focuses attention on two dimensions: the likelihood that harm may occur and the severity of the potential outcome. For studying, you can think in low / moderate / high terms; use your official course materials for CPI’s exact presentation.

ScenarioLikelihoodSeverityReasoning
A student quietly says “no” and puts their head down.PossibleLowNoncompliance may be disruptive but does not by itself indicate imminent physical harm.
A person repeatedly throws paper cups toward an empty wall.Likely to continueUsually lowFrequent behavior does not automatically equal high physical risk.
A person is swinging a heavy metal object toward nearby people.HighHighBoth probability and potential injury are substantial; safety becomes urgent.
A person calmly makes a specific credible threat with access to a dangerous means.May be uncertainPotentially severeLow emotional intensity does not eliminate serious risk; follow emergency policy.

4. Risk of behavior vs. risk of intervention

A useful professional habit is to consider both sides. An intervention can introduce its own risks: fear, injury, escalation, trauma, loss of trust, or legal/professional consequences. Therefore, “more control” is not automatically “more safe.”

Worked example: pencil case

Situation: Andy shouts, “Leave me alone!” and throws a soft pencil case onto an empty floor.

Poor reasoning: “He threw something, so he is violent.”

Better reasoning: Describe precisely: one soft object, directed at the floor, no person nearby, no immediate injury. The behavior shows escalation, but the physical risk may still be limited.

Response: Increase space, reduce audience, use calm concise communication, avoid arguing, and continue reassessing. Do not automatically jump to a restrictive intervention.

Self-check 1

A learner tears a worksheet into pieces, says “I hate this,” and sits under the desk. No one is being struck and there are no dangerous objects nearby. What should guide your first response?

Solution: Assess the actual likelihood and severity of harm, not how annoying or dramatic the behavior appears. Reduce demands/stimulation if appropriate, maintain safe space, use supportive/directive communication, and investigate the cause. The behavior does not automatically require a physical response.
Module 2

The CPI Crisis Development Model

The model pairs a person’s level of behavior with a staff approach designed to reduce further escalation.

Behavior levelWhat you may observeStaff approachMain aim
AnxietyChange from baseline: pacing, withdrawal, repeated questions, fidgeting, worry.SupportiveReduce distress early.
DefensiveArguing, refusing, challenging, blaming, verbal escalation, testing limits.DirectiveGive clear, respectful structure.
Risk BehaviorBehavior creating imminent/immediate danger to self or others.Safety InterventionsMaximize safety and minimize harm.
Tension ReductionDecrease in energy after crisis: quietness, fatigue, crying, remorse, withdrawal.Therapeutic RapportReconnect, recover, and learn.

Anxiety → Supportive

Example A: repeated reassurance seeking

Student: “Is my mom coming? What time? Are you sure? What time?”
Less helpful: “I already told you five times. Stop asking.”
More helpful: “You’re worried about pickup. The schedule says 3:00. Let’s put it on this visual card so you can check it.”

Why: The response addresses uncertainty and reduces the need to repeatedly seek reassurance.

Defensive → Directive

“Directive” means clear, respectful, and purposeful—not louder, harsher, or dominating.

Example B: refusal

Student: “No. I’m not doing math. You can’t make me.”
Power struggle: “Yes, I can. You are doing it now.”
Directive: “You need to complete part of the assignment. You can start questions 1–3 with me, or take a five-minute break and then start questions 1–3.”

Why: The expectation remains, but the student has a manageable choice and a path to regain control.

Risk Behavior → Safety Interventions

The response should be based on real-time risk and should remain reasonable, proportionate, least restrictive, and last resort. Non-restrictive environmental and verbal strategies remain important whenever they can be used safely.

Tension Reduction → Therapeutic Rapport

Example C: after the peak

After yelling and knocking over a chair, the person is now sitting quietly, breathing more slowly, looking exhausted.

Less helpful: immediately demanding an apology and a detailed explanation.

More helpful: restore calm first: “You’ve had a difficult few minutes. We can sit quietly. When you’re ready, we’ll figure out what would help next.”

Self-check 2

A person who was arguing loudly is now silent, slumped in a chair, and tearful. What is the likely level and staff approach?

Solution: Likely Tension Reduction; respond with Therapeutic Rapport. Focus first on recovery and reconnection, then debrief when the person has enough regulation and capacity to participate.
Module 3

Integrated Experience

Behavior influences behavior. You cannot fully control the other person’s response, but you can control your own contribution to the interaction.

The escalation loop

Negative loop

Person raises voice → staff feels disrespected → staff raises voice → person feels threatened → person escalates → staff becomes more controlling.

Regulating loop

Person raises voice → staff notices own tension → staff lowers voice and increases space → person receives fewer threat cues → conversation becomes more manageable.

Rational Detachment

CPI describes Rational Detachment as managing your behavior and attitude without taking another person’s actions personally. It does not mean being cold or indifferent. It means staying professional enough to choose your response.

A 10-second internal reset

  1. Notice: “My shoulders are tight; I want to argue.”
  2. Name: “I’m feeling challenged.”
  3. Reframe: “This behavior may be about distress, not about me.”
  4. Choose: lower volume, slower pace, fewer words, more space.
  5. Reassess: did risk decrease, stay the same, or increase?

Precipitating factors: what might be driving behavior?

Useful categories to investigate include pain or illness, sleep loss, hunger, trauma reminders, sensory overload, communication difficulty, unexpected changes, perceived loss of control, conflict, embarrassment, fear, or demands that exceed current ability.

Avoid mind-reading: “Attention seeking,” “manipulative,” and “lazy” often close inquiry too early. Replace labels with observable facts and testable hypotheses.

Worked example: “You’re the worst staff here!”

Your first impulse: defend yourself: “That’s not true. I’ve done everything for you.”

Rationally detached response: “You’re very frustrated with me right now. Tell me the main thing you need me to understand.”

Reasoning: You are not agreeing with the insult. You are refusing the invitation to fight and redirecting toward usable information.

Self-check 3

Which statement best reflects Integrated Experience?

A. “If I stay calm, the other person will definitely calm down.”
B. “My behavior can influence the interaction, even though I cannot guarantee the other person’s behavior.”
C. “The person in crisis controls what staff do.”

Solution: B. Integrated Experience is about mutual influence, not guaranteed control. Staying calm improves the conditions for de-escalation but does not promise a particular outcome.
Module 4

Supportive Communication Skills

Communication is not only the words you choose. The person is also receiving your posture, distance, facial expression, tone, volume, cadence, timing, and willingness to listen.

Three channels

Verbal

The actual words: clear, concrete, respectful, brief.

Paraverbal

How the words sound: tone, volume, pace, rhythm, emphasis.

Nonverbal

Posture, facial expression, gestures, space, orientation, movement.

Empathic listening: five practical moves

  1. Give attention: stop multitasking when safe to do so.
  2. Listen for the message underneath: fear? confusion? unfairness? loss of control?
  3. Reflect briefly: “You’re upset because the plan changed.”
  4. Clarify: “What part is most difficult right now?”
  5. Move toward action: “Here are the two things I can do.”

Use short, concrete language when arousal is high

Instead ofTryWhy
“You need to calm down and start making better choices because this is becoming completely inappropriate.”“Step back. I’ll give you space. We can talk when voices are lower.”Fewer processing demands.
“Why are you doing this again?”“What happened just before you got upset?”Less accusatory; more information-seeking.
“Stop being disrespectful.”“I’ll listen. I need you to speak without threatening me.”Defines the usable boundary.
“No.”“I can’t do X. I can offer Y or Z.”Pairs the limit with an alternative.

Preparing for a difficult conversation

  • Choose a time when the person is able to process.
  • Know your non-negotiable safety boundary.
  • Identify what choices are genuinely available.
  • Reduce unnecessary audience.
  • Plan your first sentence.
  • Know how you will exit or seek help if risk rises.
  • Consider sensory and communication needs.
  • Check your own emotional state first.

Worked example: changed schedule

Person: “You promised we were going to the gym! You always lie!”

Step 1 — validate the real issue: “You expected the gym, and the change is frustrating.”

Step 2 — state reality simply: “The gym is closed today.”

Step 3 — give real choices: “We can walk outside now, or use the exercise room after lunch.”

Step 4 — wait: allow processing time instead of filling silence with more persuasion.

Self-check 4

You say, “I’m here to help,” but you are standing inches away, pointing a finger, and speaking sharply. What is the problem?

Solution: Your verbal message and your paraverbal/nonverbal message conflict. Under stress, the threatening cues may dominate. Increase appropriate space, neutralize posture, lower volume, and make your behavior consistent with the words.
Module 5

Responding to Defensive Behaviors

“Defensive” is not one single behavior. The intervention should match what the person is doing now, rather than using the same response for every escalation.

The official 3rd-edition outline names the Verbal Escalation Continuum but does not list its stages. Related CPI materials commonly describe stages such as Questioning, Refusal, Release, Intimidation, and Tension Reduction. The descriptions below are a study-oriented paraphrase; use your official learner materials for exact course language.

1. Questioning

First distinguish an information-seeking question from a challenging question.

Information-seeking

“Why do I have to leave at 4:00?”

Response: answer briefly and factually.

Challenging

“Who are you to tell me what to do?”

Response: avoid debating status; redirect to the immediate issue.

Example: “You don’t have to agree with me. Right now we need a safe plan for leaving the room.”

2. Refusal

Refusal often invites a power struggle. Your job is to set a reasonable, enforceable limit while preserving as much choice as possible.

A useful limit-setting structure

State expectation→Offer choices→State outcome→Allow time

Person: “I’m not putting the tablet away.”

Staff: “Tablet time is finished. You can put it on the desk yourself, or I can hold the case while you place it inside. After it is away, you can choose music or drawing.”

Why it works better: clear expectation, limited choices, no insult, no empty threat.

3. Release

Release may involve high emotional energy: yelling, swearing, crying, or intense venting. If it is safe, avoid competing with the person’s volume. Reduce audience and stimulation, protect dignity, and wait for enough regulation before trying to solve the entire problem.

Key distinction: Venting that is loud but not dangerous is not identical to behavior that creates imminent physical risk. Continue assessing.

4. Intimidation

Threats, threatening gestures, invasion of space, or statements suggesting violence require serious attention. Do not try to “win” the confrontation. Increase safety, obtain assistance according to policy, create distance when possible, and follow organizational emergency procedures.

Never downgrade a threat just because the person becomes quiet. Risk assessment is about likelihood and severity, not only emotional volume.

5. Tension Reduction

As the person’s physical and emotional energy decreases, shift from confrontation/limit-setting toward recovery and Therapeutic Rapport. Do not keep pushing merely because you finally have the upper hand.

BehaviorHelpful directionCommon mistake
Information questionGive a concise answer.Assuming every question is defiance.
Challenging questionRedirect to the issue; avoid power struggle.Arguing about authority.
RefusalSet simple, reasonable, enforceable limits.Threatening consequences you cannot or should not enforce.
ReleaseAllow safe venting, reduce stimulation/audience, stay calm.Lecturing or shouting over the person.
IntimidationTake threats seriously; seek support; prioritize safety.Taunting, daring, or minimizing the threat.

Self-check 5

A student says, “This rule is stupid. What are you going to do about it?” What is a better response?

Solution: Treat it as a challenging question rather than an invitation to prove authority. For example: “You don’t have to like the rule. Right now, you can finish here quietly or move to the study table.” Keep the focus on behavior and available choices.
Module 6

Safety Interventions

When behavior presents imminent or immediate danger, the goal is to protect everyone while using a response that is justified by the actual risk.

Four decision tests

Last resort

Have less restrictive, reasonably available options been used or ruled out by immediate danger?

Reasonable

Would a trained professional be able to explain why this response made sense given what was happening?

Proportionate

Does the intensity of the intervention match the seriousness of the risk?

Least restrictive

Is there a safer, less restrictive way to manage the same risk effectively?

Non-restrictive safety options

Depending on the setting and policy, examples may include increasing distance, removing bystanders, clearing dangerous objects, reducing sensory stimulation, providing an exit route, changing staff communicators, calling trained support, pausing demands, using clear safety directions, or activating an emergency plan.

Worked example: escalating object throwing

Phase 1: A person throws paper onto the floor. Risk is low. Staff reduces demands and uses concise direction.

Phase 2: The person begins sweeping light objects from a table. Staff increases space and clears others from the immediate area.

Phase 3: The person reaches for a heavy object and attempts to throw it toward another person. The risk picture changes sharply; staff activates the setting’s trained safety response.

Lesson: The same person can move through different risk levels quickly. Intervention should track current risk, not a label attached to the person.

What this tutorial intentionally does not teach

It does not describe grips, releases, holds, body positioning for restraint, takedowns, or escape mechanics. The official outline assigns substantial classroom time to demonstration, practice, a Physical Skills Review Framework, restraint-risk considerations, and competency assessment. Reading a webpage cannot substitute for that process.

Self-check 6

A person is screaming and swearing but is stationary, has no weapon, is not striking anyone, and has space around them. Is loudness alone enough to justify restrictive intervention?

Solution: No. Continue to assess actual likelihood and severity of harm. Use safe verbal/environmental de-escalation as appropriate. A restrictive response must be based on risk, not on loudness, disrespect, inconvenience, or staff frustration.
Module 7

Post-Crisis: Therapeutic Rapport and Learning

The end of dangerous behavior is not the end of crisis work. Post-crisis work helps restore safety, rebuild the relationship, identify triggers and successful supports, and improve the plan for next time.

Recovery before analysis

Immediately after a peak, the person may be tired, ashamed, tearful, quiet, confused, or physiologically depleted. A detailed interrogation at that moment may be ineffective. First stabilize the environment and the relationship.

A practical debrief structure

  1. Regulate: ensure immediate safety and allow recovery.
  2. Reconnect: communicate respect; separate the person from the behavior.
  3. Reconstruct: what happened before, during, and after?
  4. Identify: triggers, needs, early warning signs, successful supports.
  5. Plan: what should the person and staff do earlier next time?
  6. Document: objective facts and required post-event information according to policy.
  7. Support staff resilience: review impact, coping, teamwork, and learning without blame.

Objective documentation

Weak / judgmentalStronger / observable
“Client went crazy for no reason.”“At 2:14 p.m., after being told the community outing was cancelled, the client shouted, pushed two plastic chairs over, and moved toward the exit.”
“Student was manipulative.”“Student asked to call home four times after being told the test would begin in ten minutes.”
“Staff had to restrain them because they were out of control.”Document the specific behaviors, observed risk, alternatives attempted, staff response, duration, monitoring, outcome, and follow-up required by policy.

Worked debrief

Before: schedule changed without warning.

Early signs: repeated questions, pacing, refusal.

What worsened it: three staff giving instructions at the same time.

What helped: one familiar staff member, visual schedule, reduced audience, 10 minutes of quiet space.

Future plan: preview schedule changes visually, designate one communicator, offer a planned break at first signs of pacing.

Self-check 7

Why is “What did we miss earlier?” often more useful than “Who caused this?” after a crisis?

Solution: The first question promotes prevention and systems learning: triggers, communication, environment, timing, unmet needs, and staff responses. The second can turn a review into blame and may obscure modifiable factors.

Safety Interventions: Disengagement and Holding Skills — what you should know conceptually

The official outline includes separate competency-based blocks for disengagement and holding skills, use of a Physical Skills Review Framework, consideration of restraint risks, and an Opt-Out Sequence. These are practical skills taught and assessed in person.

Conceptual study points

  • Physical intervention is not a punishment or behavior-management shortcut.
  • Risk must justify the intervention.
  • Less restrictive strategies remain preferable when they can safely manage the risk.
  • Staff need training, role clarity, monitoring, and organizational authorization.
  • Physical intervention carries physical and psychological risks that must be considered.
  • After any restrictive intervention, follow required monitoring, documentation, notification, and debrief procedures.
  • Your jurisdiction and organization may impose stricter rules than a training framework.
Do not practice physical crisis techniques from text or video alone. Use only skills for which you are currently trained, competent, authorized, and operating within policy and law.

If you are preparing to teach

  • Start with a realistic workplace example.
  • Ask learners to identify behavior before giving the label.
  • Ask “What is the risk?” before “What would you do?”
  • Use teach-backs rather than lecture alone.
  • Correct unsafe reasoning, not just wrong terminology.
  • Connect every module to the previous one.
  • Invite examples without allowing confidential details to identify people.
  • End scenarios with post-crisis learning, not only the peak event.

Integrated case studies with worked solutions

Case 1 — Andy refuses a transition

Background: Andy uses a visual schedule. A preferred activity ends 15 minutes earlier than expected because the room is needed for another group.

Behavior: Andy freezes, holds the iPad tightly, says “No, no, no,” and turns away.

Questions: What level? What might be precipitating it? What should staff do?

Solution: The behavior may begin at Anxiety and move toward Defensive/Refusal. A likely precipitating factor is an unexpected change/loss of predictability. Use one calm communicator, show the revised visual schedule, acknowledge the disappointment, offer two acceptable next-step choices, and allow processing time. Avoid multiple staff repeating the demand or physically taking the device merely to obtain immediate compliance.

Case 2 — “You can’t tell me what to do”

Behavior: A teenager raises their voice after being told to leave the common area: “Who do you think you are? You can’t tell me anything!”

Solution: This resembles a challenging-question/defensive pattern. Do not answer the status challenge. Redirect: “You’re upset about leaving. The common room is closing. You can go to the lounge or your room.” If they refuse, set a clear, reasonable limit. Continue assessing for movement toward threats or Risk Behavior.

Case 3 — loud but not yet dangerous

Behavior: A person paces in a large empty area, swears loudly, and complains about a cancelled appointment. They are not approaching others or damaging objects.

Solution: High intensity does not automatically equal high physical risk. Maintain distance, reduce audience, use a calm tone, allow safe venting, and offer concise practical information when the person is ready. Do not crowd the person or turn the interaction into a contest over respectful language while arousal is high.

Case 4 — risk changes

Behavior: During the same event, the person suddenly grabs a heavy chair and begins moving toward another individual while threatening to hit them.

Solution: The risk assessment changes: likelihood and potential severity are now much higher. Prioritize immediate safety, get others out of the area if possible, activate trained support/emergency procedures, and use only authorized interventions proportionate to the danger. This is no longer merely a “verbal de-escalation” problem.

Case 5 — post-crisis shame

Behavior: Twenty minutes later, the person says quietly, “I’m sorry. I ruin everything.”

Solution: Likely Tension Reduction. Use Therapeutic Rapport. Avoid shaming or over-reassuring. A grounded response might be: “That was a hard situation. We’ll work through what happened and make a plan for next time.” Later, explore early warning signs, the cancelled appointment trigger, which staff responses helped, and what can be changed.

Case 6 — staff becomes triggered

Situation: A person calls you incompetent in front of coworkers. You notice anger and an urge to “put them in their place.”

Solution: This is an Integrated Experience/Rational Detachment moment. Your priority is managing your own contribution. Slow your speech, avoid defending your reputation, redirect to the immediate concern, and if needed have another appropriate staff member take over. A handoff can be a skilled intervention, not a defeat.

Practice assessment

Try answering each question aloud before revealing the solution. A strong answer names both the concept and the reasoning.

1. What two factors are central to CPI’s Decision-Making Matrix?
Likelihood and severity. Consider both the chance of harm and how serious the outcome could be.
2. Match: Anxiety → ?
Supportive.
3. Match: Defensive → ?
Directive. Clear, respectful structure and limits—not aggression.
4. Match: Risk Behavior → ?
Safety Interventions. Match the intervention to the immediate risk, prioritizing least restrictive and proportionate responses.
5. Match: Tension Reduction → ?
Therapeutic Rapport.
6. Define Integrated Experience in practical terms.
Your behavior and the other person’s behavior influence each other. You cannot guarantee their actions, but you can deliberately manage your own response to reduce escalation.
7. What is Rational Detachment?
Managing your own behavior and attitude without taking another person’s challenging behavior personally.
8. Why is “directive” different from “controlling”?
Directive communication supplies clear boundaries, expectations, and choices while preserving dignity. Controlling behavior may rely on power, threats, humiliation, or unnecessary restriction.
9. A person says “no” but creates no immediate danger. What common error should you avoid?
Do not confuse noncompliance with high physical risk. Use appropriate limit-setting and de-escalation before considering more restrictive responses.
10. What is one sign that a limit is poorly designed?
It is unreasonable, unclear, unrelated to safety/expectations, or cannot actually be enforced appropriately. Good limits are simple, reasonable, and enforceable.
11. When can safe venting be useful?
During high emotional release when the behavior is not creating immediate danger. Staff can reduce stimulation and avoid arguing while continuing to assess risk.
12. Why should threats be assessed rather than dismissed as “just words”?
Because severity may be high even if the person is not loud or visibly agitated. Consider specificity, context, means/access, behavior, and follow your organization’s threat/emergency procedures.
13. What is the first goal in Tension Reduction?
Recovery and reconnection. Detailed problem-solving works better after enough regulation has returned.
14. Why does post-crisis documentation emphasize observable facts?
Objective facts support accurate risk review, continuity of care/support, accountability, and future prevention; judgmental labels can obscure what actually occurred.
15. What is the overall purpose of the seven modules?
To help staff understand distress behavior, recognize escalation, manage themselves, communicate effectively, assess risk, choose proportionate safety responses, and learn after crisis so future events can be prevented or reduced.

One-page mental model

Memorize these pairings

Anxiety → Supportive Defensive → Directive Risk Behavior → Safety Tension Reduction → Therapeutic Rapport

Ask these six questions in order

  1. What am I actually observing? Describe behavior without labels.
  2. What might be driving it? Look for needs, triggers, threat, communication, environment.
  3. What is the risk? Likelihood + severity.
  4. What level is the person showing? Anxiety, Defensive, Risk Behavior, or Tension Reduction.
  5. What is my own behavior doing to the interaction? Use Rational Detachment.
  6. What is the least restrictive effective response right now? Then reassess.
Exam-style reasoning sentence: “I would choose ___ because the person is showing ___, the current risk is ___, and this response is the least restrictive reasonable action likely to reduce escalation while maintaining safety.”
